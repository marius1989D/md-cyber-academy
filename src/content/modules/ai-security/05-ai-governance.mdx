The four preceding modules were about attacks and their mechanisms. This one is about the layer that decides which of those risks an organisation actually addresses, who signs off, and how you prove it to an auditor. It is less thrilling than a pickle payload and more likely to appear on the SecAI+ exam — governance is where a large share of the objectives live, because it is where AI security becomes a programme rather than a party trick.

<Callout type="key">
Governance is not paperwork for its own sake. It is the mechanism that makes the technical controls from Modules 1–4 *repeatable and accountable*. A backdoor scan you run once is a hobby; a backdoor scan mandated by a documented risk process, evidenced on every model intake, is a control. The framework is what turns the second into the first.
</Callout>

## NIST AI Risk Management Framework

The NIST AI RMF is the voluntary US framework that most organisations reach for first, because it is practical and technology-neutral. Its spine is four functions — deliberately verbs, because each is an ongoing activity, not a document:

| Function | The question it answers |
|----------|-------------------------|
| **Govern** | Who is accountable, what is our risk tolerance, and how is this culture sustained? (The cross-cutting function that underpins the other three.) |
| **Map** | What is the context and what could go wrong here — which risks actually apply to *this* system and use? |
| **Measure** | How do we assess and track those risks, quantitatively where possible? |
| **Manage** | Which risks do we treat, in what priority, and how do we respond when they materialise? |

The RMF frames trustworthy AI around properties you can hold a system against: valid and reliable, safe, secure and resilient, accountable and transparent, explainable, privacy-enhanced, and fair with harmful bias managed. Security is one property among several — a useful reminder that "AI risk" is broader than "AI security," and an exam favourite.

## The EU AI Act

Where the NIST RMF is voluntary and risk-based, the EU AI Act is **law**, and it regulates by tiering systems on the risk they pose to people:

- **Unacceptable risk** — banned outright by **Article 5**: subliminal or purposefully manipulative and deceptive techniques that materially distort behaviour and cause significant harm (5(1)(a)); exploiting vulnerabilities of age, disability, or a specific social or economic situation (5(1)(b)); and social scoring (5(1)(c)). Name the trap in that last one: the prohibition is written actor-neutrally, so it is not limited to governments — recital 31 spells out "public or private actors". "It's a private company, so the ban doesn't apply" is a wrong answer people reach for confidently.
- **High risk** — permitted but heavily obligated. The categories are enumerated in **Annex III**, and it is worth knowing the eight headings rather than gesturing at them: (1) biometrics, (2) critical infrastructure, (3) education and vocational training, (4) employment, workers' management and access to self-employment, (5) access to and enjoyment of essential private and public services and benefits, (6) law enforcement, (7) migration, asylum and border control management, (8) administration of justice and democratic processes. This tier carries the real compliance weight: risk management, data governance, logging, human oversight, and documented accuracy and robustness.
- **Limited risk** — transparency duties under **Article 50**. Two distinct obligations, and they are easy to blur: a system that interacts directly with a person must be designed so the person is informed they are dealing with an AI (unless that is obvious to a reasonably observant person), *and* providers of systems generating synthetic audio, image, video or text must mark the outputs "in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated". The first is about disclosure to a human; the second is about machine-readable provenance in the artifact.
- **Minimal risk** — the majority; largely unregulated.

<Callout type="warn">
Two things trip people up. First, the Act is **extraterritorial** — like the GDPR, it reaches any provider whose system is used in the EU, regardless of where they sit. Second, most obligations attach to the **high-risk** tier and to general-purpose model providers; a minimal-risk system is not carrying the same load. Knowing which tier a system falls in is the first real governance decision, and the one exam questions probe.
</Callout>

## How the frameworks relate

They are complementary, not competing, and this is the mental model to carry into the exam:

- **NIST AI RMF** is the voluntary *how* — the internal process for identifying and managing AI risk, adaptable to any jurisdiction.
- **EU AI Act** is the mandatory *what* — the legal obligations that apply if you operate in or into the EU.

Run the RMF's Map/Measure/Manage loop and you generate much of the evidence the Act's high-risk obligations demand. A mature programme uses the voluntary framework to satisfy the mandatory one. Other pieces you will see referenced: **ISO/IEC 42001** (a certifiable AI management-system standard, the AI analogue of ISO 27001) and the **OWASP LLM Top 10** from Module 1 as the technical checklist that sits underneath the governance layer.

## Where this lands for SecAI+

CY0-001 weights governance, risk and compliance heavily precisely because it is the part of AI security that scales across an organisation. Expect scenario questions that hand you a system and ask you to place it in an EU AI Act tier, or to name which NIST RMF function a given activity belongs to. The reliable way to answer them is to hold the two structures firmly: the four RMF functions (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage) and the four Act tiers (unacceptable, high, limited, minimal). Most questions reduce to correctly placing something in one of those eight buckets.
