Every module so far has been about standing questions — rules that ask the same thing forever and tell you when the answer changes. Hunting is what you do about the questions nobody has asked yet.

The need for it follows directly from work you have already done. Module 2 produced a coverage map with honest gaps. Module 4 produced an exclusion list, each entry a documented blind spot. Module 3 produced a list of telemetry you collect but do not alert on. **Hunting is the activity that goes looking in exactly those places** — and that framing matters, because it means hunting is not a vague aspiration for teams with spare time. It has a defined input: your own documented weaknesses.

## What hunting is, and three things it is not

Hunting is **the human-led, hypothesis-driven search for activity your automated detections would not have caught.** Three common activities get called hunting and are not:

- **Running your detections manually.** If a rule already covers it, executing the query by hand is verification, not hunting.
- **Triaging the alert queue.** That is detection working as designed. Hunting starts where the queue ends.
- **Searching for indicators from a threat report.** Useful, and worth doing — but pasting hashes and domains into a search bar is *matching*, not hunting. It only finds what someone else already found.

That last distinction is graded explicitly in David Bianco's **Hunting Maturity Model** — the same author as Module 1's Pyramid of Pain:

| Level | Name | What characterises it |
|---|---|---|
| HMM0 | Initial | Relies on automated alerting; no hunting capability |
| HMM1 | Minimal | Collects data and searches historical data for indicators from new reports |
| HMM2 | Procedural | Applies hunting procedures developed by others, with minor changes; the most common level |
| HMM3 | Innovative | Creates original procedures, and is usually the one publishing them |
| HMM4 | Leading | Successful hunts are operationalised into automated detection |

<Callout type="key">
HMM4 is the sentence to take away: **"any successful hunting process will be operationalized and turned into automated detection."**

That closes the loop of this entire course. Hunting is not an alternative to detection engineering — it is the *research function* that feeds it. A hunt that finds something and does not become a rule has to be re-run manually forever, which means it will not be re-run. The deliverable of a good hunt is usually a detection, and the measure of a hunting programme is how many of its findings became standing coverage.
</Callout>

## A framework and three hunt types

**PEAK** — "Prepare, Execute, and Act with Knowledge" — is the current working framework, from SURGe by Splunk, with David Bianco and Ryan Fetterman as primary collaborators. Its three phases are what they sound like: **Prepare** (choose the topic, research, plan), **Execute** (analyse the data), **Act** (document, automate, communicate), with knowledge feeding in throughout.

It names three hunt types, and knowing which one you are running keeps a hunt from drifting:

- **Hypothesis-driven** — form a suspicion, then use data to confirm or deny it.
- **Baseline** (also called Exploratory Data Analysis) — establish what normal looks like, then search for deviations. This is Module 5's stack counting, promoted to a method.
- **Model-Assisted Threat Hunting (M-ATH)** — use machine learning to model known-good or known-bad behaviour and find what deviates from or matches the model.

Most of your hunts will be the first two, and the second is more productive than beginners expect: you cannot recognise abnormal until you have looked hard at normal.

## Writing a hypothesis that can fail

This is the skill that separates a hunt from an afternoon of poking at logs. A usable hypothesis is **falsifiable**, **scoped to telemetry you actually have**, and **specific about what you expect to see.**

Compare:

```text
UNUSABLE
  "Are we compromised?"
  "Is there malware on the network?"
  "Look for suspicious PowerShell."

  No end state. Nothing could disprove them. No defined data source.
  These produce four hours of browsing and no conclusion.

USABLE
  "An adversary proxying execution through signed binaries (T1218) would
   run rundll32.exe with a parent process that is not explorer.exe or
   services.exe.

   Data:      DeviceProcessEvents, last 30 days
   Expect:    a small number of hosts with rare parent/child pairs
   Falsified: if every rundll32 execution has an expected parent and
              appears estate-wide with high frequency
   Timebox:   one day"
```

The usable version names the technique, the telemetry, the observable, the condition that would disprove it, and a time limit. It can end. **A hypothesis that cannot be disproved cannot be completed**, which is why so many hunts quietly fizzle rather than concluding.

## Every hunt produces something

The most damaging myth about hunting is that a hunt which finds no adversary was a waste. There are four legitimate outcomes, and only one of them involves finding an intruder:

1. **A detection.** You found a repeatable signal; operationalise it as a rule. This is HMM4.
2. **A tuning change.** You found that an existing rule was noisier, narrower or more broken than believed.
3. **A telemetry gap.** You could not test the hypothesis because the data was not collected. That is a finding with a cost attached and an owner — arguably the most valuable output, because it fixes future hunts too.
4. **A documented negative.** You tested it properly and the behaviour is not present. Write it down, with the query, the window and the date.

Outcome four is the one teams fail to record, and its value is real: it is the difference between "we do not think we have that exposure" and "we tested for it on the 14th over 90 days of data, here is the query." The second sentence is what an auditor, a board and an interviewer all actually want.

Sentinel supports this directly, which is a useful signal that it is normal professional practice rather than pedantry. A hunt has a **hypothesis state** you set to validated or invalidated, bookmarks that preserve "the specific row results, KQL query, and time range that generated the result", and programme metrics that count **validated hypotheses**, new incidents, and new analytics rules created. An invalidated hypothesis is tracked as an outcome, not an embarrassment.

## Refining a hunt in flight

Rung 4. You start the rundll32 hypothesis above. Here is what the first query returns, aggregated:

```text
InitiatingProcessFileName   FileName        hosts   executions
explorer.exe                rundll32.exe    2,140   88,402
services.exe                rundll32.exe    2,138   14,203
msiexec.exe                 rundll32.exe    1,904   9,881
winword.exe                 rundll32.exe        1        1
cmd.exe                     rundll32.exe        3        7
```

The top three are the estate being an estate — `msiexec` spawning `rundll32` is ordinary installer behaviour, and you have just learned that, which is baseline knowledge you did not have an hour ago. The bottom two rows are the hunt.

`winword.exe` spawning `rundll32.exe` **once, on one host**, is Module 5's ancestry signal at its sharpest. Note that the original hypothesis would have missed it: it said "not `explorer.exe` or `services.exe`", which would have flagged `msiexec` across 1,904 hosts and buried the single interesting row. **The hypothesis needed refining the moment you saw the data** — from "unexpected parent" to "parent that is rare for this child across the estate", which is a rarity question rather than an allowlist question.

That refinement is the hunt working correctly, not a mistake. What you must not do is refine until something looks suspicious and then declare victory — that is fishing. The discipline is to write the refined hypothesis down, state why, and re-test it as a question that could still come back empty.

<Callout type="warn">
Three failure modes worth recognising in yourself.

**Fishing.** Browsing data until something looks odd, then constructing a story around it. Everything looks odd in enough data. Without a hypothesis stated in advance, you have no way to distinguish a finding from a coincidence.

**No timebox.** Hunts expand indefinitely because there is always another query. Decide the duration in the Prepare phase and stop when it is spent, recording where you got to.

**Not writing it up.** An undocumented hunt cannot be repeated, handed over, or counted — and it teaches the organisation nothing. If the write-up feels like the boring part, remember it is the only part that survives you.
</Callout>

<Lab title="Run one hunt end to end and produce all four outcomes" host="Detection lab + Sentinel or Elastic" minutes={90}>

You are going to run a complete hunt with a written hypothesis, a timebox, and a documented conclusion — and deliberately produce every one of the four outcome types, because each teaches a different thing.

1. **Prepare: choose the topic from your own gaps.** Open the coverage map from Module 2 and the exclusion list from Module 4. Pick one technique graded "telemetry only", or one behaviour you excluded. Do not pick a technique you already have a validated detection for.
2. **Write the hypothesis** in the usable form above: technique, data source, expected observable, falsification condition, timebox. Timebox it to 90 minutes and hold yourself to that.
3. **Execute: baseline first.** Before looking for anything bad, aggregate the relevant field across your lab and read the common values. Write down two things you learned about normal that you did not know.
4. **Now look at the tail.** Rank by rarity and examine the least common values. Record your reasoning for each of the rarest five.
5. **Produce outcome 1 (detection).** Whatever the hunt found, write one query that would have caught the most interesting thing automatically. Express it as a candidate detection with an ADS-style hypothesis and response.
6. **Produce outcome 2 (tuning change).** Compare what you saw against one existing rule or exclusion. Record one specific change you would make to it based on what the data showed.
7. **Produce outcome 3 (telemetry gap).** Identify at least one thing you wanted to check and could not, because the data was absent or incomplete. Write it as a request: what to enable, on what, and what it would let you detect.
8. **Produce outcome 4 (documented negative).** Write the negative result properly: the hypothesis, the exact query, the time window, the date, and the conclusion — in a form a colleague could re-run in six months.
9. **Act: close it out.** Write a one-page hunt report with all four outcomes, and state whether the hypothesis was validated or invalidated.

**Evidence of completion:** a journal entry containing the one-page hunt report with (a) the written hypothesis including its falsification condition and timebox, (b) the two things you learned about normal during baselining, (c) your candidate detection query, (d) the tuning change, (e) the telemetry gap written as an actionable request, and (f) the documented negative in re-runnable form. This report is the single most portfolio-valuable artifact in the course: it demonstrates that you can scope a question, test it honestly, and produce output whether or not you found an adversary — which is what a hunting role actually consists of.

</Lab>
