Module 1 ended on the ADS framework's **Categorization** section — the requirement that every detection declares which adversary behaviour it addresses. This module is about what that declaration buys you: the ability to answer "what are we covered for?" with evidence instead of a feeling. It is also about the trap that question sets, because the standard way of answering it produces confident, well-presented nonsense.

## The structure, stated precisely

ATT&CK is a hierarchy, and using the words loosely is the fastest way to look junior in a review:

- A **tactic** is the adversary's *goal* — the why. Credential Access. Each has an ID like `TA0006`.
- A **technique** is *how* they achieve it. OS Credential Dumping, `T1003`.
- A **sub-technique** is a more specific means. LSASS Memory, `T1003.001`.
- A **procedure** is a *specific observed implementation* — this actor, using this tool, with these flags.

That last distinction carries the weight of the whole module. A technique is a category; a procedure is a real thing that happened. There are many procedures per technique, and your rule almost certainly detects some of them rather than the technique.

At the top level, Enterprise ATT&CK currently defines **15 tactics**, in a rough operational order from `TA0043` Reconnaissance through to `TA0040` Impact.

<Callout type="warn">
Check that number against whatever else you read, because most training material, blog posts and course slides still say **14 tactics with "Defense Evasion" as TA0005**. As of ATT&CK content **v19.1**, that is out of date. Defense Evasion has been split in two:

- **`TA0005` Stealth** — "techniques that reduce the likelihood of detection by blending in with legitimate activity", *without* modifying security controls.
- **`TA0112` Defense Impairment** — techniques that "degrade, disable, or undermine the effectiveness and trustworthiness of security controls and monitoring mechanisms" through direct interference.

Technique IDs move too. The old `T1562` Impair Defenses now resolves to **`T1685` Disable or Modify Tools** under the new Defense Impairment tactic. The lesson generalises well beyond this one change: **ATT&CK is versioned, and your mappings rot.** Pin the version your coverage map was built against, and re-check IDs against the live site rather than from memory or an old cheat sheet — this exact class of error is why.
</Callout>

## Why coverage heatmaps lie

The ATT&CK Navigator lets you colour techniques you have detections for. Green looks like progress. It is also the easiest metric in security to fool yourself with, for three compounding reasons.

**One rule does not cover a technique.** `T1059.001` PowerShell has an enormous procedure space: encoded commands, downloaded cradles, `.NET` reflection, living-off-the-land variants, alternative interpreters. A rule matching `powershell.exe -enc` colours the box green and catches a fraction of it. The box is binary; reality is not.

**Green does not mean working.** A technique is coloured because a rule exists, not because the rule fires. From Module 1: false negatives are invisible, so an untested rule and a broken rule look identical on a heatmap.

**Coverage is not the same as relevant coverage.** Colouring in techniques your adversaries never use is effort spent on the wrong axis. Nobody faces all 15 tactics equally.

<Callout type="key">
The fix is to change what the colour *means*. Instead of binary have-a-rule, grade each technique honestly:

- **None** — no telemetry, no rule.
- **Telemetry only** — the logs exist and would let you investigate, but nothing alerts.
- **Detection, unvalidated** — a rule exists; nobody has fired it.
- **Detection, validated** — the rule has been proven to fire on a real execution of the behaviour.

That scale turns a heatmap from a marketing artefact into a work queue, and it makes the honest answer sayable: *"we have telemetry for this and no alert"* is a perfectly professional statement. **"We are covered" rarely is.** The graded version is also the one that survives a question from someone who knows the framework.
</Callout>

## Prioritising with threat intelligence

If you cannot cover everything, cover what is actually used against organisations like yours. That is what **threat-informed defence** means in practice, and it is a mapping exercise:

1. Take your threat intelligence — the ATT&CK **Groups** and **Software** pages list techniques per actor and per tool, and published industry reporting such as Red Canary's annual Threat Detection Report ranks techniques by observed prevalence.
2. Build the list of techniques those sources actually name.
3. Grade *those* first, using the scale above.

The output is a defensible prioritisation: not "we cover 62% of ATT&CK", but "of the 30 techniques most reported against our sector, we have validated detections for 18, telemetry-only for 7, and nothing for 5 — and here is the plan for those 5". That is an answer that survives a board meeting and an interview equally.

## Telemetry is the real constraint

There is a reason the grading scale starts with telemetry rather than rules: you cannot detect what you do not log. ATT&CK models this explicitly through **data sources** and **data components**, which say what kind of observation a technique is visible in — process creation, command execution, network traffic flow, and so on.

Mapping backwards from that is the highest-value exercise in this module. Rather than asking "which techniques do we have rules for", ask **"which data sources do we actually collect, and what does that make possible?"** Tooling exists for this — the open-source **DeTT&CT** project scores data source quality and produces Navigator layers from it — but the reasoning matters more than the tool. Module 1's most common failure was a perfect rule over a log source nobody collected; at programme scale, that failure becomes a heatmap full of green squares built on telemetry that was never onboarded.

## Mapping one alert properly

Rung 2. Here is an alert. Map it, and note where the honest answer is uncomfortable:

```text
ALERT: Suspicious process access to LSASS
  Source:   Sysmon Event ID 10 (ProcessAccess)
  Details:  TargetImage: C:\Windows\System32\lsass.exe
            SourceImage: C:\Users\public\svc-update.exe
            GrantedAccess: 0x1410
```

The mapping is `T1003.001` OS Credential Dumping: LSASS Memory, under `TA0006` Credential Access. So far so good, and this is where most people stop.

Now the parts that make it a real mapping. **What does this rule not cover?** `T1003` has other sub-techniques — the SAM database, NTDS, cached domain credentials — none of which touch LSASS memory and none of which this rule sees. Colouring `T1003` green because of this alert would be a straightforward overstatement; only `T1003.001` has any claim to colour. And **within** `T1003.001`, procedures that read LSASS by other means — a signed driver, a kernel technique, a handle inherited from another process — evade a rule keyed on direct `ProcessAccess`. That belongs in the ADS Blind Spots section, and repeated across a programme it is the difference between a heatmap and an inventory of assumptions.

<Callout type="detect">
Two habits that keep a coverage map honest:

- **Map at the sub-technique level, and only colour what you tested.** Rolling a sub-technique detection up to its parent is the single most common way coverage gets overstated. If you have `T1003.001` validated, say `T1003.001`.
- **Version and date the map.** A coverage layer is a claim about a moment. Record which ATT&CK version it was built against — mine says v19.1 — and re-validate when the framework updates, because as the Stealth and Defense Impairment split shows, techniques get renamed, renumbered and re-parented under you.
</Callout>

<Lab title="Build a graded, threat-informed coverage map" host="Any workstation + ATT&CK Navigator (browser)" minutes={60}>

You are producing a coverage layer you could show an interviewer, built on the graded scale rather than binary colouring. Use your own detection lab from Module 1; if you have only the one detection you wrote there, that is a fine starting point — the method is the deliverable.

1. Open the [ATT&CK Navigator](https://mitre-attack.github.io/attack-navigator/) and create a new Enterprise layer. Record the ATT&CK version the layer is built against.
2. Scope it. Pick a threat profile relevant to you — one actor from the ATT&CK Groups pages, or the top techniques from a published industry report — and list the techniques it names. Aim for 15 to 30, not the whole matrix.
3. Set up four colour bands for the graded scale: none, telemetry only, detection unvalidated, detection validated.
4. Grade each technique in your list honestly. Be strict: anything you have not fired yourself is at best "unvalidated". Most of your map should not be green, and that is the correct outcome.
5. For every technique you graded "telemetry only" or better, record *which log source* provides it. If you cannot name the source, downgrade it to none — this step is where imagined coverage gets removed.
6. Take one technique you graded "detection, validated" and write down its blind spots: name at least two procedures under that technique your rule would miss.
7. Export the layer as JSON and write a short summary in the form used above: of N techniques in the profile, X validated, Y telemetry-only, Z nothing, with the top three gaps and what each would need.

**Evidence of completion:** a journal entry with (a) the exported Navigator layer JSON and the ATT&CK version it was built against, (b) your log-source mapping for every technique graded above "none", (c) the blind-spot list for one validated technique, and (d) the one-paragraph coverage summary naming the top three gaps. Part (d) is the artifact — it is the answer to "what are you covered for?" that an interviewer is actually testing, and the honest gaps in it are what make the rest credible.

</Lab>
