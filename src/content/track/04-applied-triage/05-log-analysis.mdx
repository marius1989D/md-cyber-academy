<Why asked="Here are logs from three different sources. Tell me what happened.">
This is the capstone of Phase 4 and frequently the practical exercise in a final
interview. Everything so far has been single-source: a header, a process tree, a
capture, a binary. Real incidents span all of them, and the analyst's job is to
build one timeline that a stranger could follow. The specific skill being tested
is **pivoting** - taking a fact from one source and using it to query another -
and it is the difference between reading logs and investigating.
</Why>

## The five alerts that fill a queue

Learn the shape of each, and specifically what distinguishes the benign version
from the real one.

**1. Brute force.** Many attempts against few accounts.
*Benign version:* a service account with a stale cached password, or a user after
a password change. Look for a single account, one source, and failures that stop
when someone logs in properly.
*The question:* did anything succeed?

**2. Password spray.** One password against many accounts.
*Benign version:* a misconfigured script iterating an old account list.
*Distinguishing signal:* attempts-per-account near 1, and status codes showing a
mix of valid and invalid usernames.

**3. Impossible travel.** Two sign-ins too far apart in too little time.
*Benign version:* VPN, mobile roaming, a cloud service signing in on the user's
behalf, or plain geo-IP inaccuracy. This is the highest false-positive alert in
most SOCs.
*Distinguishing signal:* device compliance, ASN type, and whether the audit log
shows anything after the sign-in.

**4. Lateral movement.** Authentication between hosts that do not normally talk.
*Benign version:* IT administration, vulnerability scanners, backup agents,
deployment tooling.
*Distinguishing signal:* is the source host and the account combination
established behaviour, and what happened on the destination afterwards.

**5. Data exfiltration.** Large or unusual outbound transfer.
*Benign version:* backups, cloud sync clients, legitimate large file sharing.
*Distinguishing signal:* direction ratio, timing, destination, and whether the
volume matches a known service's normal pattern for that host.

<Callout type="key">
Notice the pattern across all five: **the alert tells you a pattern occurred; your
job is to determine whether it is this host's normal.** Baseline comparison is the
single most reusable triage technique, and "is this new for this host or this
account?" is the question to ask first, every time.
</Callout>

## Pivoting - the actual skill

You start with one fact. Each source you touch should hand you a new fact to take
somewhere else.

```text
  Alert: unusual sign-in
     |
     +-- pivot on USER -----> other sign-ins, audit log, mailbox rules
     |
     +-- pivot on IP -------> other accounts from this IP, firewall logs,
     |                        threat intel, other tenants
     |
     +-- pivot on TIME -----> what else happened in this window, on any source
     |
     +-- pivot on HOST -----> process events, network connections, local logons
     |
     +-- pivot on ASSET ----> what does this host or user have access to
```

Work outward until new pivots stop producing new facts. That is the natural end of
a Tier 1 investigation, and it is also the point at which you either close or
escalate.

## Timestamps will try to mislead you

<Callout type="warn">
**Normalise everything to UTC before you correlate.** Windows event logs, cloud
sign-in logs, firewall syslog and application logs can each be in a different zone,
and BST versus GMT will silently shift your timeline by an hour twice a year. An
incorrect timeline is worse than no timeline - it produces confident wrong
conclusions about causality. Always state the timezone explicitly in your write-up.
</Callout>

Also watch for clock drift on devices without reliable NTP, and for logs that
record ingestion time rather than event time. If two sources disagree about
ordering by seconds, prefer the one with the more trustworthy clock and say so.

## Building the timeline

Strip each source down to: **timestamp (UTC), source, actor, action, target,
outcome.** One line per event. Sort. Read.

Most incidents become obvious the moment they are laid out this way, which is why
the discipline is worth more than any individual tool.

<div slot="model">

**First, normalise.** Source 2 is BST (UTC+1), so subtract an hour. Getting this
wrong makes the file access appear to *precede* the sign-in, which would invert
the causality entirely. This is the trap in the exercise.

**The timeline, all UTC:**

```text
09:12:04  Password spray begins from Tor exit 185.220.x.x
          34 accounts, one attempt each, all 50126     [T1110.003]
09:14:41  p.osei signs in successfully from the SAME Tor IP,
          MFA satisfied, UNREGISTERED device            [T1078.004]
09:31:18  p.osei signs in from home IP, compliant device
          (the real user, unaware)
09:47:55  LAPTOP-P-OSEI begins beaconing to 185.220.x.x
          62 connections, ~60s apart, ~1 KB each        [T1071.001]
09:22:09  (was 10:22:09 BST) network logon to FILE-SRV-02 from
          the laptop                                     [T1021.002]
09:22:11  Special privileges assigned
09:24:53  847 files read from \\FILE-SRV-02\HR-Confidential  [T1039]
09:41:02  svc_backup authenticates from the laptop -
          NOT its normal source                          [T1078]
09:41:30  Service "WinTelemetrySvc" created,
          ImagePath C:\Windows\Temp\wts.exe              [T1543.003]
10:52:14  3.8 GB single outbound flow to 45.61.x.x       [T1041]
```

**Wait - re-check the ordering.** After conversion, the FILE-SRV-02 activity at
09:22 UTC sits *before* the beaconing starts at 09:47. That is consistent: the
attacker authenticated at 09:14, moved to the file server and collected data
first, then established the C2 channel and persistence. The exfiltration at 10:52
comes last. The sequence is coherent, and it is only coherent once the timezone is
corrected.

**The story:** a password spray from a Tor exit node succeeded against `p.osei` at
09:14. The successful sign-in **satisfied MFA from an unregistered device**, which
is the token-theft signature you know from Phase 1 - the attacker phished or
replayed a token rather than defeating MFA directly. Seventeen minutes later the
real user signed in from home, unaware. The attacker used the compromised session
to reach FILE-SRV-02, obtained privileges, and read 847 files from an HR
confidential share. They then pivoted to the `svc_backup` service account -
authenticating from the wrong source host, which is the tell - established
persistence via a service pointing at `C:\Windows\Temp`, ran C2 beaconing at
60-second intervals, and exfiltrated 3.8 GB in a single flow.

**Findings ranked:**

1. **Confirmed data exfiltration of HR-confidential material.** 847 files read, 3.8
   GB out. This is potentially a notifiable personal-data breach, and that carries
   a statutory clock.
2. **Two compromised identities** - `p.osei` and `svc_backup`. The second matters
   more: a service account authenticating from a user laptop rather than
   BACKUP-01 means credential theft, and service accounts are usually
   over-privileged and rarely rotated.
3. **Persistence established** - `WinTelemetrySvc` from a temp directory. Survives
   reboot and password resets.
4. **Live C2 channel** at the time of analysis.

**Actions:**

- **Escalate immediately as a major incident with confirmed exfiltration.** Flag
  the personal-data dimension explicitly so the notification clock starts.
- **Recommend containment:** isolate LAPTOP-P-OSEI preserving memory; revoke all
  sessions and tokens for `p.osei` (a password reset alone leaves the token valid);
  disable or rotate `svc_backup` and identify everywhere it is used before
  disabling, because breaking backups during an incident is its own problem.
- **Block** both external IPs; check the audit log for OAuth grants or inbox rules
  as additional persistence.
- **Scope:** which of the 34 sprayed accounts were valid? Did any other succeed?
  Hunt the beacon interval and both IPs estate-wide, and check whether `svc_backup`
  authenticated anywhere else.
- **Determine what was in the 847 files** - that answer, not the technical
  detail, is what the business and any regulator will need.

**The transferable lesson:** the timezone conversion was the whole exercise. Every
other finding follows from a correctly ordered timeline, and an analyst who
correlates BST against UTC without noticing will confidently report a causally
impossible sequence.

</div>
