<Why asked="A user reports a suspicious email. Walk me through your analysis.">
Every SOC has a phishing queue and Tier 1 owns it. It is the highest-volume work
you will do, the most likely subject of your first practical interview task, and
the place where a specific, unfakeable skill shows immediately: reading headers.
A candidate who says "I'd check if the sender looks legitimate" and a candidate
who says "I'd read the Received chain bottom-up and check DMARC alignment between
the header From and the SPF-authenticated domain" are not in the same competition.
</Why>

## The anatomy of an email

Three sender fields exist and they are not the same. Attackers exploit exactly
this confusion:

| Field | Also called | Who sees it | Checked by |
|---|---|---|---|
| `From:` | header From, RFC5322.From | **The user** | DMARC alignment |
| `Return-Path:` | envelope sender, RFC5321.MailFrom | Nobody, normally | SPF |
| `Reply-To:` | — | Only when replying | Nothing |

The display name (`"IT Helpdesk" <attacker@evil.com>`) is free text with no
verification whatsoever. On a phone, many mail clients show *only* the display
name — which is why display-name spoofing still works in 2026 despite being the
oldest trick in the catalogue.

<Callout type="key">
`Reply-To:` pointing somewhere different from `From:` is one of the highest-value
quick checks in the whole triage. It's the mechanism behind most business email
compromise: the message appears to come from your finance director, and your
reply quietly goes to the attacker.
</Callout>

## Reading the Received chain

Received headers are prepended as the message travels, so **read them bottom-up**
— the bottom is the origin, the top is your own gateway.

```text
Received: from mx.corp.com by inbox.corp.com          ← last hop (yours)
Received: from relay.sendgrid.net by mx.corp.com      ← 
Received: from unknown (203.0.113.44) by relay...     ← origin
```

What you're looking for:

- **The originating IP and its reputation.** A hosting provider or residential ASN
  where a corporate mail system should be is a strong signal.
- **Geographic and timing inconsistencies.** A "UK bank" originating in a data
  centre elsewhere entirely.
- **Forged headers below the trust boundary.** Anything below your own
  infrastructure can be fabricated by the sender. Only headers added by systems
  you control are trustworthy, and this is a genuinely important point most
  candidates miss.

## SPF, DKIM, DMARC — and the alignment trap

These three get conflated constantly. Keep them distinct:

- **SPF** — does the sending IP appear in the DNS record of the **Return-Path**
  domain? Authorises the *envelope* sender.
- **DKIM** — is there a valid cryptographic signature over the message, verifiable
  against a public key in the signing domain's DNS? Proves integrity and origin.
- **DMARC** — requires SPF **or** DKIM to pass **and** to be *aligned* with the
  **header From** domain, then applies a policy (none, quarantine, reject).

<Callout type="warn">
**"SPF: pass" does not mean the email is legitimate.** It means the sending server
was authorised for whatever domain is in the Return-Path — and the attacker
controls that. An attacker sending from `evil.com` with a valid SPF record for
`evil.com` gets `spf=pass`, while the `From:` header displays your CEO. This is
the single most common misreading in phishing triage, and interviewers use it as a
deliberate trap. **DMARC alignment is the check that matters**, because alignment
is what ties authentication back to the domain the user actually sees.
</Callout>

Read `Authentication-Results` as a whole, not one field:

```text
Authentication-Results: mx.corp.com;
  spf=pass smtp.mailfrom=bounce.marketingtool.net;
  dkim=fail header.d=corp.com;
  dmarc=fail action=quarantine header.from=corp.com
```

SPF passed for an unrelated domain, DKIM failed for the claimed domain, DMARC
therefore failed. That is a spoof of `corp.com`, regardless of the `spf=pass`.

## URLs

Never click. Analyse:

- **Redirect chains** — the visible link is rarely the destination. Expand
  shorteners and follow the chain in a sandbox or a URL-expansion service.
- **Homoglyphs and punycode** — `xn--` prefixes decode to lookalike characters.
  `аpple.com` with a Cyrillic а is a different domain entirely.
- **Lookalikes** — `corp-secure.com`, `corp.secure-login.net`. Check the
  registrable domain, not the subdomain; anything can be a subdomain.
- **Open redirects** — a legitimate domain used as a launder:
  `google.com/url?q=evil.com`. Passes reputation checks, lands somewhere else.
- **Domain age** — a registration from three days ago is a strong signal on its
  own.
- **AiTM kits** — a credential page that proxies to the real Microsoft login,
  capturing the post-MFA token. Visually perfect. This is what you learned in the
  cloud identity step, arriving through the phishing queue.

## Attachments

Risk order, roughly: direct executables are usually blocked, so attackers use
containers and scripts.

- **Archives and disk images** — `.zip`, `.iso`, `.img`, `.vhd`. ISOs and VHDs
  historically bypassed Mark-of-the-Web, which is why they surged.
- **Shortcut files** — `.lnk` executing a bundled payload, often via `rundll32`.
- **Office with macros** — `.docm`, `.xlsm`. Much reduced since macros became
  blocked by default from the internet, but still present.
- **OneNote, HTML smuggling, SVG** — the rotating cast of container formats.
- **PDFs** — usually a delivery vehicle for a link rather than an exploit.

Detonate in a sandbox. Never on your own machine, and never open it "just to see".

<div slot="brief">

A user in accounts payable reports this. Triage it.

```text
From: "Meridian Logistics Finance" <finance@meridian-logistics.com>
Reply-To: <m.finance@outlook-secure.net>
Return-Path: <bounce@mail.trackflow-cdn.com>
To: k.patel@corp.com
Subject: RE: Outstanding invoice 44821 - updated bank details
Date: Tue, 14 Jul 2026 09:41:02 +0000

Authentication-Results: mx.corp.com;
  spf=pass smtp.mailfrom=mail.trackflow-cdn.com;
  dkim=pass header.d=mail.trackflow-cdn.com;
  dmarc=fail action=none header.from=meridian-logistics.com

Received: from mx.corp.com by inbox.corp.com; Tue, 14 Jul 2026 09:41:09
Received: from mail.trackflow-cdn.com (198.51.100.77)
          by mx.corp.com; Tue, 14 Jul 2026 09:41:07
Received: from [10.0.14.2] by mail.trackflow-cdn.com; Tue, 14 Jul 2026 09:41:02

X-Mailer: Microsoft Outlook 16.0

BODY (abridged):
  "Further to our call, please note our bank details have changed
   with effect from this week. Updated remittance form attached.
   Kindly confirm receipt so we can update your account."

  Attachment: Remittance_Update_44821.pdf  (payload: link to
              hxxps://meridian-logistics[.]docsign-review[.]com/verify)

CONTEXT
  - Meridian Logistics is a genuine supplier of ours.
  - Subject line begins "RE:" but no prior thread exists in the mailbox.
  - Domain docsign-review.com registered 6 days ago.
  - Two other users in finance received near-identical messages.
```

What is this, what is the evidence, and what actions do you take and recommend?

</div>
<div slot="model">

**This is business email compromise — specifically supplier invoice fraud — and
the intent is to redirect payments to an attacker-controlled account.** It is not
credential phishing, and the distinction changes your response.

**The header evidence, in order of strength:**

1. **`dmarc=fail` with `header.from=meridian-logistics.com`.** This is the finding.
   The email claims to be from your supplier's domain and DMARC alignment failed,
   meaning nothing authenticated for that domain. It is a spoof.
2. **The `spf=pass` and `dkim=pass` are the trap and must not reassure you.** Both
   passed for `mail.trackflow-cdn.com` — a domain the attacker controls or is
   abusing. Authentication passing for the attacker's own infrastructure tells you
   nothing about the claimed sender. This is exactly the alignment lesson.
3. **`action=none`.** The supplier publishes a DMARC policy of `p=none`, so the
   spoof was not quarantined despite failing. Worth noting as an observation about
   the supplier's posture, not a fault of your own controls.
4. **`Reply-To` points to `outlook-secure.net`**, unrelated to both the From domain
   and the Return-Path. Replies go straight to the attacker. Classic BEC plumbing.
5. **The "RE:" with no prior thread** is thread-hijack pretexting — manufacturing
   familiarity to lower scrutiny. If the supplier has genuinely been compromised,
   the attacker may have real thread context, which raises the stakes considerably.
6. **A six-day-old lookalike domain** in the payload link, using the supplier's
   name as a subdomain of an attacker-registered domain. `docsign-review.com` is
   the registrable domain; `meridian-logistics` is just decoration.
7. **Three recipients, all in finance.** Targeted, not spray. Someone knew who pays
   the invoices.

**Immediate actions (within typical Tier 1 authority):**

- Search the tenant for every recipient of the same sender, subject, or payload
  URL. Three reported does not mean three received.
- Check whether anyone clicked the link or submitted credentials, and whether
  anyone replied to the Reply-To address.
- Confirm nothing was actioned — no payment run, no bank-detail change in the
  finance system.

**Recommendations to escalate:**

- Purge the message from all mailboxes; block the payload domain, the Reply-To
  domain and the sending infrastructure.
- **Contact Meridian Logistics through a known-good phone number, not by replying.**
  If their mailbox is compromised, replying to any address in this email reaches
  the attacker.
- Alert the finance team directly, and confirm the out-of-band verification
  process for bank-detail changes is being followed. This is a process control, and
  it is the one that actually prevents the loss.

**Severity: high despite no malware.** The impact of BEC is measured in transferred
funds, and invoice fraud losses regularly run to six figures. An analyst who
downgrades this because "there's no payload" has misread what the attack is.

**What would change the assessment:** if DMARC had *passed* aligned to
`meridian-logistics.com`, this would suggest the supplier's own mail system is
compromised rather than spoofed — a different and more serious situation
requiring urgent contact with them. The alignment result is what separates those
two conclusions, which is why it is the first thing to read.

</div>
</Scenario>
