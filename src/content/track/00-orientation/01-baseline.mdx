<Why>
Every failed career change into cyber has the same shape: months spent
re-reading material that was already comfortable, while the actual gap sat
untouched until an interviewer found it in ninety seconds. You hold CEH, so you
have real knowledge — but CEH is an offensive, breadth-first, recall-heavy exam,
and a Tier 1 SOC interview probes depth in four areas it barely touches. This
step finds out which ones, before you spend a single hour studying.
</Why>

## What CEH actually gave you

Be precise about this, because both over- and under-estimating it are expensive.

**You have:** attacker vocabulary and the shape of an attack chain. Recon,
enumeration, exploitation, privilege escalation, persistence, exfiltration.
Tool names. Common vulnerability classes. The offensive mindset, which is
genuinely valuable — most Tier 1 analysts have never thought like an attacker
and are worse at triage for it.

**You do not have:** the defender's half of every one of those pairs. You know
what Kerberoasting *is*. Do you know what it looks like in event 4769, which
encryption type flags it, and why service account naming conventions matter to
the query you'd write? That gap — technique known, telemetry unknown — is the
single most common failure mode for CEH holders in blue-team interviews.

<Callout type="key">
Your target isn't to learn attacks and defences as two subjects. It's to learn
every technique **as a pair**: what the attacker does, and what it leaves behind.
Every step in this track is built that way, and interviews reward it heavily.
</Callout>

## The four gaps this track exists to close

1. **Fundamentals depth.** CEH assumes networking and Windows knowledge rather
   than teaching it. With no commercial IT history, you have no job to have
   absorbed it from by osmosis. This is gap number one and it is not optional.
2. **Query fluency.** KQL or SPL. No certificate substitutes for it, and it is
   the thing that decides whether you're useful in week one or week seven.
3. **Triage judgement.** Deciding, under time pressure, with incomplete data,
   whether a thing is real. Untestable by multiple choice, which is why this
   track uses scenario cards instead.
4. **Written output.** Ticket notes, escalations, shift handovers, incident
   reports. Underrated by candidates, weighted heavily by hiring managers.

## Run the diagnostic

Answer honestly. Guessing right on a topic you don't know produces a study plan
that skips it, and you'll find that out in an interview instead.

    opts: ["ACK", "SYN-ACK", "RST", "FIN"],
    a: 1,
    why: "SYN → SYN-ACK → ACK. Worth knowing cold because half-open scanning, SYN floods and the RST-instead-of-SYN-ACK signature of a closed port all hang off it. If this one felt uncertain, Phase 1 step 1 is mandatory rather than optional."
  },
  {
    q: "Kerberoasting is best detected by watching for:",
    opts: ["Failed logons from a single source", "TGS requests (4769) for service accounts using RC4 encryption", "New local administrator accounts", "Outbound DNS on a non-standard port"],
    a: 1,
    why: "Kerberoasting requests a service ticket and cracks it offline. The detection sits on 4769 with encryption type 0x17 (RC4) against accounts with SPNs — because modern clients should be negotiating AES, so RC4 is the anomaly. This is exactly the attack-known/telemetry-unknown gap: CEH teaches the attack, the SOC needs the event ID."
  },
  {
    q: "You are given 300 alerts on a shift and 40 of them are the same benign scheduled task. The correct Tier 1 response is:",
    opts: ["Close all 40 quickly and move on", "Close them and raise a tuning request with the evidence that they are benign", "Escalate all 40 to Tier 2", "Leave them open for the next shift"],
    a: 1,
    why: "Closing without feeding back means the next analyst does the same 40 tomorrow. Escalating benign noise burns Tier 2. The answer that gets you hired — and promoted — is closing them *and* documenting why, so the rule gets tuned.     a: 1,

  },
  {
    q: "An analyst writes: 'Closed, false positive.' What is wrong with it?",

    a: 1,
    why: "The next person — or you, in three months, in a post-incident review — needs to know *why*. What did you check, what did it show, what would have changed your mind. Ticket writing is a graded part of BTL1 and a real part of the job, and 'closed, FP' is the single most common thing that gets junior analysts marked down."

    a: 1,
    why: "Display names are trivially forged. The Received chain shows the actual path, and Authentication-Results carries the SPF/DKIM/DMARC verdicts. Header analysis is a guaranteed interview topic for any SOC role that handles a phishing queue — which is all of them."

    opts: ["File hash", "IP address", "Domain name", "Tools, tactics and procedures"],

Forcing an adversary to change how they operate is expensive and slow, which is why behavioural detections outlive indicator feeds. Expect to be asked to explain this and justify why you would still bother with IOC matching at all."

## Read your result

<Callout type="key">
**6–8 correct:** your fundamentals are better than you think. Move fast through
Phase 1, treat it as verification rather than learning, and get to Phase 3 early.

**3–5 correct:** the expected result for a CEH holder with no commercial
experience. The plan works as written.

**0–2 correct:** also completely fine, and better to know now. Add two weeks to
Phase 1 and do the reading before the labs rather than alongside them.
</Callout>
