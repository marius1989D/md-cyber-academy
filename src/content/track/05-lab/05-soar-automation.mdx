<Why asked="What would you automate in a SOC, and what would you never automate?">
The second half of that question is the one that matters. Anyone can list things a
playbook could do; the candidate who can articulate why account disablement should
sit behind an approval while threat-intel enrichment should not is demonstrating
judgement about blast radius and authority - the same lane awareness from Phase 2,
expressed in automation. This step also closes the loop on your lab: detections
that fire, enriched automatically, ready for a human decision.
</Why>

## The two kinds of automation

Keep these separate in your head and in your answers:

**Enrichment** adds context to an alert. It changes nothing in the environment, is
fully reversible because there is nothing to reverse, and its worst failure mode
is an unhelpful comment on a ticket. **Automate freely.**

**Response** changes something - disables an account, isolates a device, blocks an
IP, deletes a message. It has business impact, may be difficult to reverse, and
its worst failure mode is an outage you caused. **Gate behind a human, or scope
extremely tightly.**

<Callout type="key">
The principle to state in an interview: *"Automate the gathering, gate the
deciding."* An analyst opening an incident should find the threat intel lookups,
asset criticality, user context and related alerts already attached. What they
should not find is an account already disabled because a rule with a 40 percent
false positive rate fired at 3am.
</Callout>

## Enrichment worth building

Ordered by return on effort:

1. **Threat intel lookup** - query reputation for any IP, domain or hash entity on
   the incident and post the result as a comment.
2. **Asset context** - is this host a domain controller or a print server? Pull
   criticality from a watchlist or CMDB and adjust severity accordingly.
3. **Identity context** - department, manager, privileged roles, recent password
   change. Turns "an account" into "a privileged finance account".
4. **Related alerts** - has this entity appeared in other incidents in the last 30
   days? Recurrence changes the picture entirely.
5. **Geolocation and ASN** - hosting provider, Tor exit, residential.
6. **Historical baseline** - has this user signed in from this country before? The
   first-seen question, answered before the analyst asks it.

Each of these is a lookup an analyst would otherwise do by hand, several times a
shift. Six of them is easily five minutes saved per alert, which across a queue is
substantial.

## Response actions and how to gate them

| Action | Blast radius | Recommended gating |
|---|---|---|
| Add a ticket comment | None | Fully automatic |
| Tag or reclassify an incident | None | Fully automatic |
| Notify a Teams channel | None | Fully automatic |
| Assign an owner | None | Fully automatic |
| Block a hash across the estate | Low, reversible | Automatic with a confidence threshold |
| Block an IP at the perimeter | Medium | Approval, or automatic only for high-confidence intel |
| Force a password reset | Medium, user-visible | Approval |
| Isolate a device | High | Approval - and selective isolation, not full |
| Disable an account | High | Approval |
| Delete mail from mailboxes | High, irreversible | Approval |

<Callout type="warn">
**Never automate an irreversible action on the output of a rule you have not
measured.** You know from Phase 2 what an untuned rule's false positive rate looks
like. Wiring that to account disablement means one noisy Tuesday locks out forty
people, and the SOC's automation is switched off permanently by an angry service
owner. Automation that gets disabled has negative value - it cost you effort and
now nobody trusts it.
</Callout>

## Approval patterns

Logic Apps supports an approval step - post to Teams or email with Approve and
Reject buttons, wait for a response, then branch. This is the pattern that makes
higher-impact automation safe:

```text
Incident created
   │
   ├─▶ Enrich (parallel, no gating)
   │     threat intel · asset context · identity · related alerts
   │
   ├─▶ Post enriched summary to the SOC channel
   │
   └─▶ IF severity == High AND confidence == High
         │
         └─▶ Post approval request with the evidence
               │
               ├─ Approved ──▶ isolate device (selective)
               │                 comment the action and approver
               │
               └─ Rejected ──▶ comment the rejection and reason
                                 assign to analyst
```

Two details that matter: **the approval request must carry the evidence**, so the
approver can decide without opening the console, and **both branches must comment
the outcome and who decided**, so the audit trail survives.

<Lab title="Build an enrichment playbook" host="Sentinel in your lab workspace" minutes={240}>

1. **Create a playbook** (Logic App) triggered on Sentinel incident creation.
2. **Extract entities** from the incident - use the entities dynamic content, and
   filter for IP and Account types.
3. **Add a threat intel lookup.** A free option that works without a paid
   subscription is fine here - the point is the pattern, not the feed. Handle the
   no-result case gracefully.
4. **Add asset context** by looking up the host against a watchlist you created in
   the Sentinel architecture step.
5. **Compose a readable summary** and add it as an incident comment. Format it so
   an analyst can read it at a glance - this is a real skill and badly formatted
   automation output gets ignored.
6. **Conditionally adjust severity** - if the asset is marked critical in the
   watchlist, raise the incident severity and tag it.
7. **Create an automation rule** that runs the playbook on incident creation for
   your detection rules.
8. **Test it** by firing an atomic that triggers one of your Phase 5 detections.
   Watch the incident appear, then watch it enrich itself.
9. **Measure it.** Time yourself triaging an enriched incident versus an
   unenriched one. Record the difference - that number is your evidence.

**Evidence to capture:** a screenshot of an enriched incident, the playbook
designer view, and your before-and-after triage timing. Log it.

</Lab>

<Scenario

  title="Design the automation for a phishing incident"
  minutes={10}
>
<div slot="brief">

An MSSP asks you to specify the automation for their phishing queue. Current
state: 60 to 80 reported phishing emails per day, each taking an analyst 12 to 20
minutes. Two Tier 1 analysts per shift.

```text
CURRENT MANUAL WORKFLOW
 1. Open the reported message, extract headers
 2. Check SPF/DKIM/DMARC results
 3. Look up sender domain reputation and registration age
 4. Extract URLs, check reputation, expand redirects
 5. Extract attachment hashes, check reputation
 6. Search the tenant for other recipients of the same message
 7. Check whether anyone clicked the link
 8. Decide: benign / spam / phishing / confirmed malicious
 9. If malicious: purge from mailboxes, block sender and URLs,
    check for post-compromise activity on any clicker
10. Reply to the reporting user
11. Write the ticket

AVAILABLE: Defender for Office 365, Sentinel, Logic Apps,
           a commercial threat intel feed, Teams
```

Specify what you automate fully, what you gate, and what stays manual. Justify
each boundary.

</div>
<div slot="model">

**Automate fully - steps 1 through 7, all enrichment, none of it changes
anything.**

These are lookups. Every one is deterministic, reversible in the sense that
nothing is altered, and each is a place a tired analyst makes copy-paste errors at
4am.

- **1-2 Header and authentication parsing.** Pure extraction. Post the From,
  Return-Path, Reply-To and the full Authentication-Results as a formatted comment,
  and **explicitly flag DMARC alignment failure** rather than just dumping the raw
  string - the automation should surface the finding, not the data.
- **3-5 Reputation lookups.** Sender domain age and reputation, URL reputation and
  expanded redirect chain, attachment hash reputation. Handle "no result" as a
  distinct outcome from "clean", because they are not the same and conflating them
  is how automation misleads.
- **6-7 Scoping.** Query `EmailEvents` for other recipients of the same
  `NetworkMessageId` or campaign, and `UrlClickEvents` for who clicked and whether
  they clicked through. **This is the highest-value automation in the whole
  workflow** - it is the slowest manual step and it produces the four numbers an
  incident manager asks for first.

Post all of it as a single structured comment. An analyst should open the incident
and have the entire picture already assembled.

**Automate with a confidence gate - step 8, partially.**

Auto-classify only at the extremes, and only where the evidence is unambiguous:

- **Auto-close as benign** where DMARC passes aligned, the sender domain is
  established and well-reputed, no URLs or attachments, and no clicks. This is a
  meaningful share of the queue and it is safe because the failure mode is a missed
  phish that a user already thought was suspicious - which is bad, so set the
  threshold conservatively and sample-audit the auto-closures weekly.
- **Auto-escalate to high severity** where multiple reputation sources flag the
  payload and someone clicked through. Escalating is not destructive.
- **Everything in between stays manual.** Which is most of it, and that is correct.

**Gate behind approval - step 9, all of it.**

- **Purge from mailboxes is irreversible.** A wrongly purged legitimate email -
  an invoice, a contract, a job offer - is a real business impact and the user
  cannot get it back. Approval, always, with the evidence in the request.
- **Blocking sender domains and URLs** is medium impact and reversible, but a
  wrongly blocked supplier domain stops legitimate business mail and the failure is
  discovered slowly, by someone else, when a deal goes quiet. Approval, or
  automatic only where the intel confidence is very high and the domain is
  newly-registered.
- **Post-compromise checks on anyone who clicked** should be automated as
  *enrichment* - run the queries, attach the results - but any resulting containment
  is a separate decision and follows the normal escalation path.

**Stays manual - steps 10 and 11.**

- **Replying to the reporting user.** A templated auto-reply acknowledging receipt
  is fine and worth doing. But the substantive response should be human, because
  reporting users are the detection layer you most want to keep engaged, and a
  wrong or robotic answer trains them to stop reporting. That behavioural effect is
  worth more than the minutes saved.
- **The ticket.** The analyst's reasoning is the point of the ticket, and
  automation cannot supply it. The enrichment comment gives them the facts; the
  assessment must be theirs.

**Expected result:** 12 to 20 minutes down to perhaps 4 to 6 for the cases that
still need a human, with a share of the queue auto-closing safely. Two analysts
can now handle the volume without pattern-closing, which was the actual risk.

**How I would introduce it:** enrichment first, running for two weeks with nobody
acting on it automatically, while measuring accuracy. Then auto-close at a
deliberately conservative threshold, audited weekly. Approval-gated response last.
**Deploying response automation before you have measured your enrichment's accuracy
is how SOCs end up with automation that everyone has quietly disabled.**

</div>
