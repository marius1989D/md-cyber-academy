<Why asked="Why should we hire you over someone with a computer science degree and an internship?">
Every interview you sit will contain a version of this question, and the answer
you give determines whether your background is treated as a deficit to be excused
or a difference to be valued. Candidates who apologise for their history invite
the interviewer to agree. Candidates who explain what it gave them that a graduate
does not have change the frame entirely - and the honest truth is that a
manufacturing technician on a 24/7 rota has genuinely relevant experience a
graduate has never had.
</Why>

## The frame

Three things you have that a typical graduate applicant does not:

**1. You have done the job's actual rhythm.** Tier 1 SOC work is monitoring a
baseline, triaging alarms, distinguishing genuine faults from nuisance trips,
diagnosing root cause under time pressure, escalating with documented steps, and
handing over cleanly at end of shift. You have done exactly that shape of work, in
a setting where mistakes stopped a production line. A graduate has done none of it.

**2. You have proven you can sustain shift work.** MSSPs lose Tier 1 analysts to
night rotas constantly. You have already demonstrated, over years, that you can
work them without your life falling apart. This is a genuine hiring risk you
remove, and almost nobody says it out loud.

**3. You have documented to audit standard.** IATF 16949 and ISO 9001 records are
scrutinised by external auditors. Ticket discipline is the thing junior analysts
are most often criticised for, and you have been doing the equivalent under
external scrutiny for years.

<Callout type="key">
The compressed answer: *"A graduate knows the theory better than I did two years
ago. What I bring is that I have already done this shape of work - monitoring a
baseline, triaging alarms, diagnosing root cause under time pressure, and
documenting it to audit standard on a 24/7 rota. And I closed the technical gap
deliberately: CEH, BTL1, SC-200, and a detection lab I built, attacked and wrote
detections against. I'd rather show you the detections than argue about the
degree."*
</Callout>

## What not to do

**Do not apologise.** "I know I don't have the traditional background, but..." is
a sentence that concedes the point before you make yours.

**Do not oversell.** Claiming your factory work was secretly security work is
untrue and collapses under one question. The transferable claim is about the
*shape* of the work, and it is strong enough on its own.

**Do not lead with passion.** "I've always been fascinated by hacking" is what
every applicant says and none get remembered for. Lead with what you built.

**Do not disparage your old job.** Speaking dismissively about manufacturing makes
an interviewer wonder how you will speak about them. It was skilled work; you are
changing direction, not escaping.

## Building the STAR bank

Structure every behavioural answer as **Situation, Task, Action, Result** - and
keep Action longest, because it is the part being assessed.

You need six to eight stories, drawn from both the lab and the shop floor. The
prompts they must cover:

| Prompt | Best source |
|---|---|
| A time you solved a difficult technical problem | Lab - the detection that missed two atomics |
| A time you worked under pressure | Shop floor - a line-down fault |
| A time you made a mistake | Either - the ingestion cap, or a production error |
| A time you had to explain something technical to a non-technical person | Either |
| A time you disagreed with someone | Shop floor - a process or safety call |
| A time you learned something quickly | Lab - KQL, or the SC-200 sprint |
| A time you improved a process | Either - tuning a rule, or a setup improvement |
| A time you handled a repetitive task without losing attention | Shop floor - directly relevant to alert fatigue |

<Callout type="warn">
**Have a genuine mistake story and do not sanitise it.** "My biggest weakness is
that I care too much" is transparent and irritating. A real error, what it cost,
what you changed afterwards, and what has happened since is far stronger - and it
is one of the few questions where honesty is measurably the winning strategy.
</Callout>

<Scenario
  step="track/07-campaign/04-narrative"
  id="star-mistake"
  title="Build the mistake story"
  minutes={8}
>
<div slot="brief">

You will be asked "tell me about a time you made a mistake". You have two
candidate stories:

```text
OPTION A - Shop floor
  Ran a production batch without verifying the material dryer
  had completed its cycle. Parts came out with splay defects.
  Around 400 parts scrapped, roughly two hours of line time
  lost. Nobody was hurt, no customer received bad parts.

OPTION B - Lab
  Set a Sentinel daily ingestion cap and then forgot about it.
  Spent an afternoon convinced a detection was broken and
  rewriting the query, when in fact ingestion had stopped
  because the cap was hit. Lost about three hours.
```

Which do you use, and how do you structure it?

</div>
<div slot="model">

**Use A, the shop floor story. It is the stronger answer for a reason worth
understanding.**

Option B is a good story and you should keep it - it demonstrates the pipeline
diagnostic lesson well. But it cost three hours of your own time in a lab where
nothing was at stake. **Option A had real consequences: scrap cost, lost line
time, and a process you were responsible for.** Interviewers are assessing whether
you can own a genuine failure, and a story with no real cost reads as a safe
answer chosen to avoid the question.

There is also a second-order benefit: telling a manufacturing story here quietly
demonstrates that your previous career involved genuine responsibility, which
reinforces the whole narrative frame without you having to argue for it.

**The structure:**

**Situation (brief).** *"On a night shift I was running a batch on a machine I'd
set up dozens of times. Material has to be dried before moulding or it produces
splay defects."*

**Task.** *"I was responsible for verifying the setup was correct before starting
the run."*

**Action - the longest part, and where the assessment happens.** *"I skipped the
dryer verification because the cycle usually finished well before I needed it, and
I assumed it had. It hadn't. About four hundred parts came out defective before I
caught it on a routine quality check. I stopped the line immediately, quarantined
the batch, logged it, and told my supervisor rather than waiting for the morning
handover - the quality system depends on that being reported at the time, not
retrospectively."*

**Result, and this is the part that matters.** *"Around four hundred parts scrapped
and about two hours of line time. No bad parts reached the customer, because the
quality process caught it. What I changed afterwards was the specific thing: I
stopped relying on 'it usually has by now' as a verification and started
physically confirming the dryer cycle state as an explicit step. I also raised it
at the shift meeting, because I wasn't the only one making that assumption - and
the setup sheet was updated to make it a checked item."*

**Then close the loop to the role, in one sentence.** *"It's the same failure mode
as assuming a data source is reporting because it usually is. In my lab I lost an
afternoon to exactly that - a detection I thought was broken turned out to be an
ingestion cap. Now I confirm telemetry is arriving before I conclude anything about
what it shows."*

**Why that closing line is worth the seconds:** it uses the lab story as
supporting evidence rather than as the main answer, links a manufacturing lesson
to a security habit, and shows the same lesson learned twice in two domains - which
is considerably more convincing than either story alone.

**What makes the whole answer work:**

- A real cost, stated plainly, with no minimising
- You caught it, stopped it, and reported it immediately rather than concealing it
- The fix was specific and behavioural, not "I'll be more careful"
- You extended the fix beyond yourself by raising it and getting the procedure changed
- You connected it to the job you are applying for without straining

**What to avoid:** blaming the dryer, the shift pattern, or the setup sheet. The
answer is about ownership, and every deflection weakens it.

</div>
</Scenario>

**This post has a different job from the other three.** Those demonstrate
technical capability to a hiring manager. This one is written to be *shared* - it
is the post that reaches people who would never read a KQL tutorial, and career
changers, recruiters and managers all engage with this genre.

**Two warnings.** Do not make it a plea - a post that reads as "please hire me"
gets sympathy, not interviews. And do not disparage manufacturing; the respectful
version is stronger and more widely shared.

**Expect this to be the post an interviewer has read** before you walk in. Write
it so you are happy to be asked about any sentence in it.
