<Why>
A meaningful share of UK entry-level SOC hiring happens through recruiters running
keyword searches and through people who already recognise your name. Applying cold
through a portal puts you in a stack of two hundred; being found, or being
remembered, puts you in a conversation. This step is not about becoming a content
creator - it is about being findable by the right search and credible when someone
lands on your profile.
</Why>

## How recruiters actually find you

LinkedIn Recruiter search matches keywords against your **headline, job titles,
skills and About section** most heavily. That has three practical consequences:

1. **Your headline must contain the words a recruiter types.** "SOC Analyst",
   "Security Analyst", "Microsoft Sentinel", "KQL", "Threat Detection".
2. **Your current job title is a manufacturing one**, which no security search
   will match. You cannot lie about it - but you can add a second, honest position
   for your own project work, which is legitimate and searchable.
3. **The Skills section is a literal keyword list.** Fill it with the technologies
   from the adverts you are targeting.

<Callout type="warn">
**Do not invent a job.** Adding "Security Analyst at [made-up company]" is
fabrication and will end a process when checked. What is legitimate: adding your
own project or brand as a self-employed or project entry, described accurately as
independent lab and research work. The distinction is whether the work described
actually happened - it did.
</Callout>

## The headline

The most valuable 220 characters on your profile. It appears in every search
result, every comment, every connection request.

**Weak:** *"Injection Moulding Technician at [Company]"* - accurate, invisible to
every security search.

**Weak:** *"Aspiring Cyber Security Professional | Passionate about Blue Team |
Seeking Opportunities"* - no searchable technology, and "aspiring" and "seeking"
both signal the thing you least want emphasised.

**Strong:** *"SOC Analyst (CEH, BTL1) | Microsoft Sentinel, KQL, Defender XDR |
Detection Engineering | Career changer from manufacturing"*

Certifications, three searchable technologies, a discipline, and the career change
stated plainly rather than apologetically. Owning it is stronger than hiding it,
and it filters out employers who would have rejected you at that hurdle anyway.

## The About section

Five short paragraphs, first person, no third-person biography voice.

1. **What you do now**, in one line - the same factual framing as your CV summary.
2. **The evidence** - the lab, the detections, the numbers, the certifications.
3. **The background**, honestly translated - process monitoring, alarm triage,
   documented escalation under a 24/7 rota.
4. **What you are looking for** - Tier 1 SOC, MSSP or in-house, shift work
   acceptable, SC clearance eligible, location and commute radius.
5. **Contact** - portfolio link, GitHub, email.

The first two lines are what shows before "see more" is clicked, so put the
strongest content there.

## Featured section

Underused and highly effective. Pin three things:

- Your best write-up - blog post 2 or 3
- Your GitHub portfolio repo
- Your BTL1 credential

These render as cards with images and are the first thing a visitor's eye goes to
after the headline.

## Posting - the honest version

You do not need to post daily. You need to be visible enough that your name is
familiar to a few people who work in UK SOCs.

**A sustainable rhythm:**

- **Publish each blog post as a LinkedIn article** (native, not just a link -
  LinkedIn suppresses reach on posts containing external links). Four posts across
  the campaign is enough.
- **Comment substantively two or three times a week** on posts by UK practitioners.
  Not "Great post!" - a specific observation, a question, or an addition. This is
  where recognition actually accumulates, and it costs five minutes.
- **Post occasionally about what you are building.** A detection you tuned, a gap
  you found, a technique you tested. Two or three sentences and a screenshot.

<Callout type="key">
**Commenting well is more effective than posting for someone with no audience.**
A thoughtful comment on a post with two thousand views reaches more relevant people
than a post to your forty connections. It also puts you in front of the author -
frequently someone senior in the field - repeatedly, which is how people come to
recognise your name.
</Callout>

## Connections

Aim for a few hundred relevant connections, not thousands of anyone.

**Who to connect with:** UK SOC analysts and managers, MSSP recruiters, people who
post useful blue-team content, others on the same career-change path, and speakers
from meetups you attend.

**How:** always with a note. Two sentences, specific, no ask.

> *"Hi [name] - I read your post on tuning Sentinel analytics rules and it
> matched what I found building detections in my own lab, particularly the point
> about scoped exclusions. Changing career into security from manufacturing and
> finding your posts useful."*

That gets accepted. "I'd like to add you to my professional network" mostly does
not.

## Beyond LinkedIn

- **BSides events** across the UK - Cheltenham, London, Leeds, Manchester and
  others. Cheap, welcoming, and genuinely useful for meeting people who hire.
- **Local meetups and DC groups.** Milton Keynes puts you in reach of London,
  Oxford, Cambridge, Northampton and Birmingham.
- **CyberSecurity communities on Discord and Slack** - the Blue Team Village and
  various UK-focused servers.

Attending three events over the campaign and following up with the people you meet
is worth more than three hundred additional connections.

<div slot="brief">

Four candidate headlines. Rank them for a recruiter searching LinkedIn for entry-level
SOC candidates in the UK, and explain the reasoning.

```text
A  "Injection Moulding Technician at [Company] | CEH Certified"

B  "Aspiring Cyber Security Analyst | Passionate about protecting
    organisations | Open to opportunities | #cybersecurity #infosec"

C  "SOC Analyst (CEH, BTL1) | Microsoft Sentinel, KQL, Defender XDR |
    Detection Engineering | Career changer from manufacturing"

D  "Cyber Security Professional | 10+ years experience | Expert in
    threat detection and incident response"
```

</div>
<div slot="model">

**Ranking: C, A, B, D.**

**C is clearly strongest.** It contains four searchable technology keywords
(Sentinel, KQL, Defender XDR, detection engineering) plus two certifications, and
a recruiter's search for "Sentinel KQL SOC" matches it directly. Crucially it
states the career change plainly rather than hiding it - which both pre-empts the
obvious question and filters out employers who would reject on that basis anyway.
The claim "SOC Analyst" without a company reads as a professional identity rather
than a job title, which is accepted convention and not a fabrication.

**A is second, which may be surprising.** It is honest and contains one useful
keyword (CEH). It will rarely surface in a security search because the dominant
terms are manufacturing ones - but if someone does land on it, nothing there
damages credibility. Weak reach, no downside.

**B is third and worse than it looks.** "Aspiring" and "Open to opportunities"
both emphasise the absence of a role, which is the thing you least want leading.
"Passionate about protecting organisations" is unverifiable and universal. The
hashtags do almost nothing in headline position. Most importantly it contains **no
searchable technology at all** - a recruiter searching for Sentinel or KQL skills
will never see it. It reads as effort without direction.

**D is worst, and it is worst because it is dishonest.** "10+ years experience"
and "Expert in threat detection and incident response" are false. This will
surface in searches, generate calls, and collapse in the first two minutes of the
first conversation - and the recruiter who discovers it will not forward you for
anything else. A single overclaim on a public profile can end a relationship with
an agency that covers a large share of the roles you want. The reputational cost
in a small national market is real and lasting.

**The general principle:** a headline needs to be *searchable*, *credible*, and
*honest*, in that order of what people usually get wrong. C achieves all three. B
fails searchability. D fails honesty, which is the only unrecoverable failure of
the four.

</div>
