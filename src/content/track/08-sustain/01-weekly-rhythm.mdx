<Why asked="How do you keep your skills current?">
Two things happen to people who reach this point. The first is that the search
takes longer than expected and skills quietly decay while they wait - so the
interview in month five goes worse than the one in month two would have. The
second is that they land the job, stop learning, and are still Tier 1 four years
later wondering why. Both are avoided by the same thing: a small weekly routine
that does not depend on motivation. This step is that routine, and it does not
end when you get hired.
</Why>

## The rhythm

Four items. Roughly four hours a week, which fits around shift work.

| Item | Time | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **One intrusion report** | 45 min | Read a DFIR Report or vendor write-up. Cover the conclusion, build the timeline yourself, then compare. |
| **One challenge** | 90 min | A CyberDefenders or LetsDefend case, or a TryHackMe room. Hands on keys, not reading. |
| **One detection or query** | 45 min | Write something. A rule, a hunt, a Sigma conversion. Small is fine; consistency is the point. |
| **One batch of applications** | 60 min | During the campaign. After you land, this becomes one hour of deliberate learning instead. |

<Callout type="key">
**Consistency beats intensity, and it is not close.** Four hours every week for six
months is roughly a hundred hours of deliberate practice. Two heroic weekends
followed by a month of nothing is forty hours and no retention. Build the rhythm
around your rota - the same two evenings and one rest-day morning, every cycle -
so it survives the weeks when you cannot face it.
</Callout>

## Skill decay is real and specific

Some things fade faster than others, and the fast-fading ones are exactly what
gets tested:

- **Query fluency goes first.** KQL syntax you could write from a blank editor in
  week 18 takes twice as long by week 30 if you have not touched it. This is why
  the weekly query matters more than it looks.
- **Event ID recall fades quickly** without use. Keep the flashcard deck and run it
  monthly.
- **Triage judgement holds up better**, because it is reasoning rather than recall -
  but it degrades without exposure to real artifacts.
- **Concepts and frameworks barely decay.** ATT&CK, the IR lifecycle and the
  Pyramid of Pain stay with you.

The practical consequence: if you get an interview after a quiet month, spend the
two days before it on queries and event IDs specifically, not on re-reading
concepts you already know.

## Staying current

Being able to reference something published this month is cheap interview currency
and almost nobody bothers. Fifteen minutes a day covers it:

- **A daily brief** - Simply Cyber's daily threat brief or equivalent
- **Detection Engineering Weekly** - the newsletter, once a week
- **The DFIR Report** when they publish
- **Vendor threat blogs** - Microsoft, Huntress, Red Canary, Sophos X-Ops
- **NCSC advisories** for UK-specific relevance

<Callout type="warn">
**Curate ruthlessly and accept that you will miss things.** The volume of security
content is unbounded and trying to keep up with all of it produces anxiety rather
than knowledge. Five sources read properly beats fifty skimmed, and nobody in this
field is across everything - the people who look like they are have simply chosen
their scope and stopped apologising for it.
</Callout>

<div slot="model">

**First, read the funnel properly, because it is telling you something specific
and it is not what it feels like.**

Eight phone screens, three technical interviews and a final stage from 340
applications is **not a broken campaign**. A roughly 14 percent response rate at
entry level is respectable, and reaching a final stage means your CV, your
narrative and your technical answers all worked well enough to get you to the last
room. The feedback confirms it: you lost to commercial experience, not to a gap in
your capability or your presentation.

**So the top of the funnel is functioning. The problem is conversion at the
end - and one identifiable thing has changed.**

**The actual diagnosis: the campaign has decayed, and it happened quietly.**

- The weekly rhythm has halved. That means twenty weeks of skill decay in exactly
  the areas that fade fastest - query fluency and event ID recall - going into
  interviews that test them.
- Nine weeks without a blog post means your public evidence has been static since
  roughly month three, while your competition's has not.
- Two agencies have gone quiet, which usually means you have dropped down their
  list rather than that they have no roles.

None of that was a decision. It is what happens when a long campaign runs on
motivation instead of routine, and it is the most common way searches fail from a
position of strength.

**What I would change, in priority order:**

**1. Restore the rhythm first, before anything else.** Not because it feels
productive, but because you have three interviews' worth of evidence that you can
reach the final stage - and the thing most likely to have degraded since then is
the technical fluency you demonstrate there. Two evenings and one morning per rota
cycle, fixed.

**2. Attack the one thing the feedback actually named.** "Commercial SOC
experience" is the stated reason, and you cannot manufacture it - but you can
narrow the gap. Two moves:

- **Escalate the side-door tier.** The MSP service desk roles from your Phase 7
  Tier 4 list are no longer a hedge; at month five they are the highest-expected-
  value applications on your list. Nine to twelve months of commercial IT at a
  company that runs a SOC converts the exact objection you just lost on.
- **Prioritise defence and cleared roles harder.** They are the tier most willing
  to train and vet rather than requiring arrival-ready experience, and the long
  vetting timeline you started in month one may now be working in your favour.

**3. Publish again, and publish the right thing.** Nine weeks of silence is
visible. The post to write now is not another detection walkthrough - it is
something that demonstrates continued momentum. A write-up of a recent DFIR
Report, mapped and analysed. It is a weekend's work and it restores the signal
that you are still building.

**4. Re-engage the quiet agencies deliberately.** Not "any updates?" - send them
something new. The new post, a new certification, an updated CV. A candidate with
news is a candidate worth resubmitting; a candidate checking in is an inbox item.

**5. Ask the final-stage employer one specific question.** You reached the last
round, so someone there formed a real opinion of you. "I understand the decision -
would you be willing to tell me the one thing that would most improve my chances
next time?" People answer this surprisingly often, and it is the single most
valuable feedback available to you. Also ask to be kept in mind; entry-level roles
recur, and being the known near-miss is a strong position.

**On the motivation, honestly.** Twenty weeks of mostly silence while working full
shifts is genuinely wearing, and treating that as a character failing rather than
a predictable response to the situation makes it worse. Two things help
concretely: measure yourself on effort rather than outcomes, which you control -
and do not do the whole of this alone. A peer on the same path, a community, or
simply telling someone how it is actually going takes a surprising amount of the
weight off. If it is affecting your sleep or your health more broadly, that is
worth taking seriously rather than pushing through.

**The thing to hold onto:** you got to a final stage. Most people applying to
these roles do not. The evidence says this works - it has just not finished
working yet.

## The first 90 days

When you land - and the plan is built so that you do - the first three months set
your trajectory more than the next year does.

**Weeks 1-4: absorb.** Learn their stack, their runbooks, their escalation
criteria, their customers. Ask questions freely; this is the only period where
asking a lot is expected rather than noted. Write things down.

**Weeks 5-8: contribute quietly.** Take the tickets. Write good notes. Be the
analyst whose handovers are clear. Reputation at Tier 1 is built almost entirely
on documentation quality and reliability, which is exactly the ground you already
stand on.

**Weeks 9-12: improve one thing.** Find a noisy rule and bring a measured tuning
proposal - fire rate, false positive categorisation, a scoped intervention, the
reasoning. You have done this in a lab; doing it once in production, properly, is
how a new starter gets noticed for the right reasons.

<Callout type="key">
**Do not arrive intending to fix everything.** New analysts who spend their first
month explaining what is wrong with the SOC are correct roughly half the time and
resented approximately always. Learn why things are the way they are first - there
is usually a reason, and if there is not, you will be far more persuasive in month
three than in week two.
</Callout>

## Beyond Tier 1

Know roughly where you are heading, because it shapes what you learn:

- **Tier 2 / senior analyst** - deeper investigation, mentoring, the natural next
  step in 18-24 months.
- **Detection engineering** - you already have a head start; this is the most
  direct route from your portfolio.
- **Threat hunting** - hypothesis-driven work, usually after Tier 2.
- **Incident response / DFIR** - deeper forensics; BTL1 into GCFA or equivalent.
- **Cloud security** - fast-growing, and your Entra and Azure work points at it.
- **Threat intelligence** - if the analytical and writing side appeals more than
  the technical.

You do not need to choose now. But noticing which parts of this track you enjoyed
most is real information - and for what it is worth, the detection engineering
work in Phase 5 is where your portfolio is strongest.

## Closing

You started this track with CEH, no commercial security experience, and a
manufacturing job. If you have worked through it properly you now have: a
detection lab you built and attacked, a documented technique-to-telemetry map,
twelve or more validated detections with measured tuning, portable Sigma rules
with one submitted upstream, enrichment automation, four published write-ups, a
public portfolio, SC-200, BTL1, a rehearsed narrative, and a tracked campaign.

That is not a career changer hoping to be given a chance. That is a candidate with
evidence.

The part that decides the outcome now is not knowledge - it is persistence past
the point where the silence gets discouraging, which is exactly what the weekly
rhythm above is for.

Good luck. Go and get the job.
