<Why asked="You've decided an alert is a real incident. Walk me through what happens next.">
Phase 1 taught you to read evidence. This phase teaches you what to *do* with it,
and the honest answer for Tier 1 is narrower than most candidates assume — you
detect, analyse, document and escalate, and you recommend containment rather than
performing it. Candidates who claim they'd isolate hosts and reset credentials on
their own authority get marked down, because it shows they don't understand where
the decision rights sit. Knowing your lane precisely is itself a hiring signal.
</Why>

## The framework everyone actually uses

NIST SP 800-61 defines four phases, and the arrows matter as much as the boxes:

```text
   ┌─────────────────┐
   │ 1. Preparation  │◀──────────────────┐
   └────────┬────────┘                   │
            ▼                            │
   ┌─────────────────────┐               │
   │ 2. Detection &      │◀────┐         │
   │    Analysis         │     │         │
   └────────┬────────────┘     │ loop    │
            ▼                  │         │
   ┌──────────────────────────┴──┐       │
   │ 3. Containment, Eradication │       │
   │    & Recovery               │       │
   └────────┬────────────────────┘       │
            ▼                            │
   ┌──────────────────────────┐          │
   │ 4. Post-Incident Activity├──────────┘
   └──────────────────────────┘
```

Two things people get wrong:

- **Phase 3 loops back to phase 2.** Containment reveals new evidence, which
  drives more analysis, which reveals more to contain. It is not a one-way street,
  and describing it as one is a tell that you've only read the diagram.
- **Phase 4 feeds phase 1.** Lessons learned change the preparation — new
  detections, new runbooks, new logging. An IR process with no feedback loop just
  repeats the same incident.

<Callout type="key">
SANS teaches the same thing as six steps — **PICERL**: Preparation,
Identification, Containment, Eradication, Recovery, Lessons Learned. It's the
same process with containment split out. Either is fine to cite; know both names
so a question phrased in the other vocabulary doesn't wrong-foot you.
</Callout>

## Event, alert, incident — the words matter

Used loosely in conversation, precisely in interviews:

- **Event** — any observable occurrence. A logon. Billions per day. Not inherently
  interesting.
- **Alert** — an event or pattern that matched a detection rule. Hundreds per day.
  Most are not incidents.
- **Incident** — a confirmed or suspected violation of security policy that
  warrants response. A handful.

Tier 1's job is the funnel between the second and the third. Getting the funnel
right in both directions is the skill: false negatives let real attacks through,
false positives burn Tier 2 and destroy the SOC's tolerance for your escalations.

## Severity, priority, and why they differ

**Severity** is how bad this is if it's real. **Priority** is what you work first.
They aren't the same, and confusing them is why queues get triaged badly.

A rough functional-impact model:

| | Low information impact | High information impact |
|---|---|---|
| **Low functional impact** | Routine — batch it | High — sensitive data at risk |
| **High functional impact** | High — business disrupted | **Critical — drop everything** |

Priority then adjusts for two more things: **how time-sensitive the response is**
(an active encryption event decays in minutes; a month-old dormant webshell does
not), and **how many assets are affected**.

<Callout type="warn">
The most common Tier 1 prioritisation mistake is working the *loudest* alert
rather than the most *time-critical* one. A thousand-event brute force that is
still failing can wait twenty minutes. A single successful authentication
following it cannot.
</Callout>

## Order of volatility — why sequence matters

If evidence is being collected, it goes from most to least perishable:

1. CPU registers, cache
2. **RAM** — running processes, network connections, injected code, keys
3. Network state — connections, ARP, routing
4. Running processes and open files
5. Disk
6. Remote and archived logs
7. Physical configuration, backups

The Tier 1 relevance is narrow but real: **don't recommend a reboot or a reimage
before someone has decided whether memory needs capturing.** "Turn it off and on
again" destroys everything in the top three tiers. Suggesting isolation rather
than shutdown is the right instinct — it cuts the attacker off while preserving
the volatile evidence.

## What Tier 1 actually decides

Be precise about your lane, because interviewers test it:

**You do:** triage the alert, gather supporting evidence, determine whether it's
a true positive, assign an initial severity, document your reasoning, escalate
with a clear write-up, and *recommend* containment actions.

**You generally don't:** isolate production hosts, disable accounts, block at the
firewall, or contact the customer — unless your SOC has explicitly delegated
those actions with a runbook that says so. Many MSSPs do delegate some of them,
which is why the correct interview answer is *"I'd escalate with a containment
recommendation, and take the action myself if it's within my delegated authority
under the runbook."* That sentence shows you understand the concept **and** that
the answer is organisation-specific.

## Triage the queue

<Scenario
  step="track/02-defensive-core/01-ir-lifecycle"
  id="queue-priority"
  title="03:40 — four alerts, one of you"
  minutes={8}
>
<div slot="brief">

You are the only Tier 1 on shift. Four alerts land within two minutes of each
other. Tier 2 is on-call but waking them has a cost — you're expected to use
judgement.

```text
[A]  Severity: HIGH    "Brute force detected"
     FIN-APP-03 · 4,120 × event 4625 over 40 min, still ongoing
     All failures. Source: 91.x.x.x (external, blocked at edge)

[B]  Severity: MEDIUM  "Suspicious PowerShell"
     WKSTN-1188 · encoded command, parent excel.exe
     Fired 90 seconds ago. Host still online.

[C]  Severity: CRITICAL "Malware detected — quarantined"
     WKSTN-0435 · EICAR test file in Downloads
     EDR action: quarantined successfully

[D]  Severity: LOW     "Impossible travel"
     m.reeves@corp.com · London 03:12 → Dublin 03:31
     Both sign-ins successful, both MFA satisfied, both managed devices
```

Order them, justify the order, and say what you do with each.

</div>
<div slot="model">

**Order: B, D, A, C.** The stated severities are almost inverted, and that's the
point of the exercise — severity labels come from the rule author, priority comes
from you.

**B first — Suspicious PowerShell, MEDIUM.** This is the only alert showing an
*active, unresolved* compromise. `excel.exe` as the parent of an encoded
PowerShell command is macro-based initial access; nothing legitimate looks like
this. It fired 90 seconds ago and the host is still online, so the attacker is
plausibly still executing. **Time-criticality, not label severity, puts this
first.** Investigate immediately: decode the command, pull the child process tree,
check for LSASS access and outbound connections. If it confirms, escalate and
recommend isolating the host.

**D second — Impossible travel, LOW.** Rated low, but it is a *successful
authentication* anomaly, and successful auth anomalies outrank failing ones. That
said, read the detail: both sign-ins MFA-satisfied, both from managed compliant
devices, London to Dublin in 19 minutes. That's a VPN egress change or a
geo-IP artefact far more often than an attack — a real AiTM would typically show
an unmanaged device or an unusual ASN. So: check the audit log for OAuth consents,
inbox rules and MFA method changes, confirm the device IDs match the user's known
devices, and if it's clean, close it *with that reasoning written down*. Ten
minutes of work, and it's second because a successful login you haven't explained
is a worse thing to leave sitting than a failing one.

**A third — Brute force, HIGH.** 4,120 failures sounds alarming and is the noisiest
thing on the screen. But every attempt **failed**, and the source is **already
blocked at the edge**. The control worked. What actually matters is the single
question: *did any attempt succeed?* Query for a 4624 from that source or against
those accounts. If none, this is a monitoring note and a possible tuning
candidate, not an incident. Volume is not severity.

**C last — Malware detected, CRITICAL.** EICAR is the industry-standard *test*
file. It is deliberately harmless and exists precisely to verify that AV is
working. It was quarantined successfully. This alert is evidence the control
functions. Close it, and note who dropped it — usually IT testing, occasionally
a curious user. If EICAR alerts are firing as CRITICAL regularly, that's a tuning
request.

**The transferable principle:** rank by *time-criticality × certainty of
compromise*, not by the label. Ask of every alert: is something bad happening
**right now**, and does the evidence show a control **failed** or **held**?
Failures that were blocked are noise; successes you can't explain are the job.

**On waking Tier 2:** escalate B if the process tree confirms execution beyond the
PowerShell. Don't wake anyone for A, C or D. Being trusted to make that call is
most of what separates a good Tier 1 from a pager relay.

</div>
</Scenario>
