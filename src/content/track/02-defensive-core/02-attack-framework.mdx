<Why asked="What's the difference between a tactic and a technique? Map an attack you've read about.">
ATT&CK is the shared language of modern defence. Detections are tagged with it,
escalations reference it, threat intel is organised by it, and coverage is
argued about in it. Using it fluently — technique IDs in your ticket, correct
tactic attribution, an honest view of what your coverage claim actually means —
is a fast, unfakeable signal that you've operated rather than just studied. Using
it *wrongly*, which mostly means treating it as a checklist, is an equally fast
signal in the other direction.
</Why>

## The hierarchy, precisely

Four levels, and interviewers do check that you can separate them:

- **Tactic** — the adversary's *goal*. Why they're doing it. `TA0006` Credential
  Access. There are 14 in the Enterprise matrix.
- **Technique** — *how* they achieve it. `T1003` OS Credential Dumping.
- **Sub-technique** — a more specific how. `T1003.001` LSASS Memory.
- **Procedure** — the actual observed implementation. "APT29 used a renamed
  procdump.exe with `-ma lsass.exe` to write a minidump to `C:\Windows\Temp`."

<Callout type="key">
The one-line answer: *"A tactic is the adversary's objective — the why. A
technique is the method used to achieve it — the how. A procedure is the specific
implementation a particular actor used. Credential Access is the tactic, OS
Credential Dumping is the technique, LSASS Memory is the sub-technique, and
'renamed procdump writing a minidump' is the procedure."*
</Callout>

## The 14 Enterprise tactics, roughly in order

They aren't strictly sequential — real intrusions loop — but this ordering is how
an attack tends to unfold, and knowing it lets you spot which stage an alert
belongs to:

```text
 1  Reconnaissance          TA0043   gathering information
 2  Resource Development    TA0042   building infrastructure
 3  Initial Access          TA0001   getting in
 4  Execution               TA0002   running code
 5  Persistence             TA0003   staying in
 6  Privilege Escalation    TA0004   getting more rights
 7  Defense Evasion         TA0005   avoiding detection
 8  Credential Access       TA0006   stealing credentials
 9  Discovery               TA0007   learning the environment
10  Lateral Movement        TA0008   moving to other systems
11  Collection              TA0009   gathering target data
12  Command and Control     TA0011   communicating out
13  Exfiltration            TA0010   stealing data
14  Impact                  TA0040   destroying, encrypting, disrupting
```

**A single technique can serve several tactics.** Valid Accounts (T1078) is
simultaneously Initial Access, Persistence, Privilege Escalation and Defense
Evasion, because using legitimate credentials achieves all four. Understanding
that the mapping is many-to-many, not one-to-one, is what separates working
knowledge from memorised structure.

## The parts of ATT&CK people never open

Most candidates know the matrix. Fewer know these, and they're where the value is:

- **Data Sources** — each technique lists the telemetry that reveals it (Process
  Creation, Command Execution, Logon Session). This is the bridge from ATT&CK to
  your logging architecture: it tells you *what to collect* to be able to detect a
  given technique at all.
- **Detection guidance** — concrete advice per technique. Often the fastest route
  from "I need to detect X" to a working idea.
- **Mitigations** — preventive controls, numbered `M####`. Useful when an
  escalation asks "how do we stop this recurring?"
- **Groups and Software** — `G####` and `S####`. Which actors use which techniques.
  This is how threat intel becomes a prioritisation input rather than a newsletter.

## Navigator, and the coverage trap

**ATT&CK Navigator** is a free web tool for painting layers on the matrix — what
you can detect, what an actor uses, where the gaps are. Building a coverage layer
for your own lab is a genuinely impressive portfolio artifact.

<Callout type="warn">
**"We cover 80% of ATT&CK" is a meaningless statement**, and saying it in an
interview is a trap you can avoid. Three reasons: techniques aren't equally
weighted (nobody cares equally about all 200+); "covered" is undefined — one
brittle rule matching a tool name is not coverage; and the matrix keeps growing,
so your percentage moves without you doing anything. The mature framing is
*"we have tested detections for the techniques most relevant to the threats we
actually face, and we know which of those detections are behavioural versus
signature-based."*
</Callout>

## Related models, in one paragraph each

**Cyber Kill Chain** (Lockheed Martin) — seven linear stages from reconnaissance
to actions on objectives. Older, simpler, still widely cited. Its weakness is the
linearity: real intrusions loop and skip. Useful for explaining an incident to
non-technical stakeholders; ATT&CK is what you use operationally.

**Diamond Model** — every event has four vertices: adversary, capability,
infrastructure, victim. Pivot from any one to find the others. Genuinely useful
for intel analysis and for structuring how you pivot during an investigation.

**D3FEND** — MITRE's defensive counterpart, mapping countermeasures to techniques.
Newer and less used, but naming it shows you're current.

<div slot="brief">

A condensed intrusion narrative, of the kind you'd find in a DFIR write-up. Map
each numbered action to a **tactic and technique**, then answer the question at
the end.

```text
1. A finance user receives an email with an ISO attachment. Opening it
   mounts a drive containing a shortcut file.

2. The shortcut executes rundll32.exe against a bundled DLL.

3. The DLL creates a registry value under
   HKCU\...\CurrentVersion\Run pointing to a copy of itself in AppData.

4. It queries the domain for all computers and all users via LDAP,
   and runs `whoami /groups` and `net group "Domain Admins" /domain`.

5. It requests Kerberos service tickets for 12 accounts with SPNs,
   all with RC4 encryption.

6. Three hours later, an admin account authenticates to FILE-01
   over SMB and copies a toolset to C:\Windows\Temp.

7. Data from three file shares is compressed into a single
   password-protected archive.

8. The archive is uploaded over HTTPS to a cloud storage provider.

9. Ransomware is deployed via a Group Policy Object to 400 hosts.
```

Map all nine. Then: **which single detection, placed as early as possible, would
have had the greatest effect — and why?**

</div>
<div slot="model">

**The mapping:**

| # | Tactic | Technique |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Initial Access | T1566.001 Phishing: Spearphishing Attachment |
| 2 | Execution / Defense Evasion | T1204.002 User Execution: Malicious File; T1218.011 Signed Binary Proxy Execution: Rundll32 |
| 3 | Persistence | T1547.001 Registry Run Keys / Startup Folder |
| 4 | Discovery | T1018 Remote System Discovery; T1087.002 Domain Account Discovery; T1069.002 Domain Groups |
| 5 | Credential Access | T1558.003 Steal or Forge Kerberos Tickets: Kerberoasting |
| 6 | Lateral Movement | T1021.002 Remote Services: SMB/Windows Admin Shares; T1570 Lateral Tool Transfer |
| 7 | Collection | T1560.001 Archive Collected Data: Archive via Utility |
| 8 | Exfiltration | T1567.002 Exfiltration to Cloud Storage |
| 9 | Impact | T1486 Data Encrypted for Impact; delivered via T1484.001 Group Policy Modification |

Note step 2 spans two tactics — rundll32 is both *how the code ran* and *how it
evaded* naive detection, since it's a signed Microsoft binary. That many-to-many
relationship is the thing to demonstrate you understand.

**The highest-value early detection: step 2 — rundll32 executing from a
user-mounted ISO, with an unusual parent.**

Why this one rather than an earlier or a louder stage:

- **Earlier isn't better if it isn't reliable.** Detecting step 1 means detecting
  phishing generically, which is a mail-security problem with an unavoidable false
  negative rate. You can't build a SOC detection that catches all malicious
  attachments.
- **Step 2 is the first point where the attacker must run code on your endpoint**,
  and it produces high-fidelity telemetry: a process-creation event with a parent
  that has no business spawning rundll32, from a mounted removable-style volume.
  The ISO-to-shortcut-to-rundll32 chain has almost no legitimate equivalent.
- **Everything downstream depends on it.** Persistence, discovery, Kerberoasting,
  lateral movement and ransomware all require this foothold. Cutting here costs
  the attacker the entire intrusion; cutting at step 9 costs them a redeploy.
- **The later stages are individually detectable but progressively more expensive
  to respond to.** Kerberoasting at step 5 is a good detection and you should have
  it — but by then credentials are already being cracked. GPO modification at step 9
  is an excellent detection, and it fires roughly ninety seconds before you have a
  400-host encryption event.

**The honest caveat:** you want detections at *several* of these stages, not one.
Defence in depth exists because any single detection can be evaded. The correct
interview framing is "step 2 gives the best ratio of fidelity to blast-radius
reduction, but I'd want independent coverage at 5, 6 and 9 because an attacker who
evades rundll32 detection still has to Kerberoast, still has to move laterally, and
still has to deploy."

</div>
</Scenario>
