<Why asked="A rule is generating 40 false positives a day. What do you do?">
Most of your shift will be spent on things that turn out to be nothing. New
analysts experience that as failure — as if the real job were interrupted by
noise. It isn't: the noise *is* the job, and the analysts who progress are the
ones who treat every false positive as a tuning input rather than a chore. This
step is also where a Tier 1 candidate can distinguish themselves sharply, because
almost nobody arrives able to describe a tuning methodology.
</Why>

## What a SIEM actually does

Four functions, in order:

1. **Aggregate** — collect logs from everywhere into one queryable store.
2. **Normalise** — map disparate formats to a common schema (the previous step).
3. **Correlate** — apply logic across events, sources and time to produce alerts.
4. **Present** — dashboards, incidents, investigation and case management.

Correlation is the part that earns the licence fee. A single failed logon means
nothing. Fifty failed logons across fifty accounts from one IP inside ninety
seconds, followed by one success, is a story — and only correlation across events
and time can tell it.

## Rule types

| Type | Fires on | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Signature / atomic** | A single matching event | Known-malicious hash executed |
| **Threshold** | Count over a window | >20 failed logons in 5 min from one IP |
| **Aggregation** | A grouped statistical property | One IP against >10 distinct accounts |
| **Correlation** | Sequence across sources | Failures then success, then privileged logon |
| **Anomaly / baseline** | Deviation from learned normal | User signs in from a country never seen |
| **First-seen** | Something never observed before | First RDP between this host pair |
| **Threat intel match** | Indicator from a feed | Connection to a known C2 IP |

First-seen and aggregation rules give the best value for effort at entry level:
cheap to write, hard for attackers to avoid, and they don't depend on an
indicator feed staying current.

## The confusion matrix, and the trade-off it forces

|  | Actually malicious | Actually benign |
|---|---|---|
| **Alert fired** | True Positive ✓ | **False Positive** — noise |
| **No alert** | **False Negative** — miss | True Negative ✓ |

Two derived measures do the real work:

- **Precision** — of everything that fired, how much was real. Low precision means
  alert fatigue.
- **Recall** — of everything real, how much fired. Low recall means misses.

<Callout type="key">
**You cannot maximise both.** Tightening a rule to cut false positives will always
risk missing edge cases; loosening it to catch everything floods the queue. The
correct framing in an interview is that the right balance is *severity-dependent*:
for a ransomware-encryption detection you accept poor precision because a miss is
catastrophic. For an informational rule you demand high precision because nobody
will read it otherwise. Saying "it depends on the cost of a miss versus the cost
of the noise" is the answer.
</Callout>

## Alert fatigue is a systems problem

An analyst facing 300 alerts a shift, 290 of them noise, will start
pattern-closing — recognising the shape of the usual false positive and closing it
in seconds without really looking. Eventually a real one wearing that shape gets
closed too. This is not laziness; it is the predictable output of the system, and
it is how real breaches get missed by SOCs that were technically monitoring.

Which is why "close it and move on" is the wrong Tier 1 instinct and "close it
with evidence and raise a tuning request" is the right one. You are the only
person who sees the noise; if you don't report it, nobody fixes it.

## A tuning methodology

The thing almost no candidate can describe. Learn this sequence:

**1. Measure before you touch anything.** How often does it fire, over what period,
and what proportion is genuinely false? "It's noisy" is not a case; "fires 42
times a day, 40 from the same three service accounts, zero true positives in 60
days" is.

**2. Find the *shared* property of the false positives.** Not the individual
instances — the thing they have in common. Same parent process? Same service
account? Same subnet? Same time window? This is the whole skill.

**3. Choose the narrowest intervention that removes them:**

- **Exclusion / allowlist** — exclude the specific benign condition. Precise, but
  every exclusion is a hole an attacker could hide in, so scope it tightly. Exclude
  *"this parent process, on this host, with this command line"*, never *"this host"*.
- **Threshold change** — raise the count or lengthen the window. Simple, and blunt:
  it also raises what an attacker must stay under.
- **Add a condition** — require a second signal before firing. Usually the best
  option, because it improves precision without creating an exclusion hole.
- **Change the logic entirely** — sometimes the rule is detecting the wrong thing
  and should be rewritten against behaviour instead.
- **Lower the severity** — if it's genuinely informational, stop paging on it and
  send it to a dashboard.
- **Fix the environment** — occasionally the right answer isn't a detection change
  at all. If a misconfigured script causes the noise, fix the script.

**4. Document the change and why.** An undocumented exclusion is a permanent,
invisible blind spot that nobody dares remove because nobody remembers why it's
there.

**5. Re-measure, and set a review date.** Exclusions should expire. What was
benign in March may be an attacker's chosen hiding place by September.

<Callout type="warn">
The dangerous tuning move is excluding by host or by user, because that is exactly
what an attacker who has compromised that host or user needs. If you must exclude
broadly, note the risk explicitly and time-limit it.
</Callout>

## Metrics you'll hear quoted

- **MTTD** — mean time to detect. Compromise to alert.
- **MTTA** — mean time to acknowledge. Alert to an analyst picking it up.
- **MTTR** — mean time to respond, or resolve — ask which, they differ.
- **Alert volume per analyst per shift** — the fatigue proxy.
- **True positive rate** — the health-of-tuning proxy.

Be a little sceptical of these in interviews. A SOC can improve MTTA by closing
alerts faster without investigating them, and can improve its true positive rate
by deleting rules that fire. Metrics drive behaviour, including bad behaviour.
Noticing that is a mature observation, and interviewers notice people who make it.

  title="Tune a rule that fires 42 times a day"

>
<div slot="brief">

You've been given a noisy rule to fix. It's the first tuning task you've been
trusted with, so it's also an audition.

```text
RULE:  "Encoded PowerShell execution"
LOGIC: DeviceProcessEvents
       | where FileName == "powershell.exe"
       | where ProcessCommandLine has_any ("-enc", "-e ", "-encodedcommand")
SEVERITY: High · pages the on-call

60-DAY STATS
  Total fires:        2,517   (~42/day)
  True positives:     1        (day 12, confirmed macro dropper)
  False positives:  2,516

FALSE POSITIVE BREAKDOWN
  1,890  Parent: ccmexec.exe      Host: all workstations
         CommandLine contains "-EncodedCommand" + base64 that decodes
         to an SCCM inventory script. Signed, scheduled, identical daily.

    412  Parent: MonitorAgent.exe Hosts: 40 servers
         Decodes to a health-check script. Identical every 15 min.

    198  Parent: explorer.exe     Host: WKSTN-0912 only
         User: k.mercer (IT engineer). Varied commands, mostly
         admin one-liners. Ad-hoc, business hours.

     16  Parent: winword.exe / excel.exe   Various hosts
         Varied. Includes the day-12 true positive.
```

Tune it. Say exactly what you'd change, in what order, and what you'd refuse to do.

</div>
<div slot="model">

**Read the breakdown before touching the rule.** The four groups are qualitatively
different and need different treatment — the lazy fix is one broad exclusion, and
it would destroy the rule's value.

**Group 1 — SCCM (1,890 fires, 75% of the noise).** Machine-generated, identical
daily, signed parent, decodes to a known inventory script. **Exclude, narrowly.**
Not "exclude ccmexec.exe" — exclude *parent is `ccmexec.exe` **and** the command
line matches the known inventory pattern*. That way, if an attacker ever
compromises SCCM and pushes something different through it, the rule still fires.
Excluding the parent alone would hand them a perfect hiding place, and SCCM is a
genuinely attractive target precisely because it can execute on every workstation.

**Group 2 — monitoring agent (412 fires).** Same treatment, same reasoning: parent
**and** command-line pattern, on the known server set. Time-limit it and review in
six months.

**Group 3 — the IT engineer (198 fires).** This is the interesting one and the
place a junior analyst goes wrong. **Do not exclude by user or by host.** An IT
engineer's workstation is a high-value target and their account is privileged;
excluding them creates the single most attractive blind spot in the estate. The
commands are *varied*, so there's no stable pattern to exclude on either.

What to do instead: this is legitimate but risky behaviour that should be visible
without paging anyone. **Lower its severity and route it to a dashboard** — split
the rule so that encoded PowerShell from an interactive parent on a known admin
workstation is Informational, while everything else stays High. And raise it as an
environment finding: the engineer could use script files or JEA instead of encoded
one-liners, which would remove the noise at source. That's tuning the environment
rather than the detection, and it's the better fix where it's available.

**Group 4 — Office parents (16 fires, including the only true positive).**
**Change nothing here, and if anything raise it.** `winword.exe` or `excel.exe`
spawning encoded PowerShell has no legitimate explanation, and this group produced
the one real detection in 60 days. Consider promoting it to its own separate rule
at Critical severity — it's a far higher-precision detection than the parent rule,
and separating it means the good signal is no longer diluted by the noise.

**What I'd refuse to do:**

- **Disable the rule.** It caught a real dropper. One true positive in 60 days
  sounds poor until you consider what the alternative outcome was.
- **Exclude by host or user.** Explained above — that's building the attacker's
  hiding place for them.
- **Raise a threshold.** Encoded PowerShell is an atomic detection; a threshold
  would only mean an attacker who runs it once gets a free pass.

**Expected result:** roughly 42/day down to around 1–2/day at High severity, with
the true positive still caught, plus a new Critical rule with excellent precision
and a dashboard of admin activity that stays visible without waking anyone.

**Document all of it** — each exclusion, its scope, the reasoning, and a review
date — and re-measure after two weeks to confirm the noise actually went and
nothing else broke.

</div>
</Scenario>
