## What the assessment is

A hands-on incident investigation against a live environment, conducted over a
24-hour window, culminating in a written report. You are not expected to work for
24 continuous hours - the window is generous precisely so you can sleep, eat and
think.

**The report is the deliverable.** Finding things is necessary but not sufficient;
the marks come from evidencing what you found, structuring it, and drawing
defensible conclusions. Everything in Phase 4 step 6 applies directly, and this is
where that step earns its place in the track.

## Pacing strategy

The single biggest failure mode is spending eighteen hours investigating and three
hours writing. Plan the opposite shape.

```text
Hours 0-1     Read everything. Do not touch a tool yet.
              Understand what is being asked, what artefacts
              exist, and what the deliverable must contain.

Hours 1-3     Broad sweep. Establish the scope: which hosts,
              which accounts, roughly what window. Do not go
              deep on the first interesting thing you find.

Hours 3-8     Deep investigation. Follow the pivots. Capture
              evidence as you go, not afterwards.

Hours 8-10    BREAK. Sleep if it is overnight. Genuinely.

Hours 10-16   Second pass. Fill gaps, corroborate findings
              across independent artefacts, chase loose ends.

Hours 16-21   WRITE. Start the report while you still have
              time to go back and get anything missing.

Hours 21-23   Review, verify every claim against evidence,
              tidy formatting, check the timeline.

Hour 23-24    Buffer. Submit early.
```

<Callout type="key">
**Start writing at the two-thirds point, not the end.** Writing surfaces the gaps
in your investigation - you discover you never confirmed a timestamp, or cannot
evidence a claim you were about to make. Discovering that with five hours left is
recoverable. Discovering it with forty minutes left is not.
</Callout>

## Evidence discipline

Capture as you go. Reconstructing evidence at hour twenty is how people run out
of time.

- **Screenshot everything the moment you find it.** Include enough context in the
  frame that the screenshot is self-explanatory later - the query, the hostname,
  the timestamp.
- **Keep a running timeline file from minute one.** Timestamp, source, actor,
  action, target, outcome. The six-column format from Phase 4 step 5.
- **Record the query or command that produced each finding.** A finding you cannot
  reproduce is a finding you cannot defend.
- **Note what you checked and found nothing**, as well as what you found. Negative
  results belong in the report and demonstrate thoroughness.
- **Normalise timestamps to UTC immediately** and state it. The Phase 4 lesson.

## Writing the report

Use your six-section template. Adapted for an assessment:

1. **Executive summary** - what happened, impact, in plain language
2. **Timeline** - UTC, chronological, one line per event
3. **Detailed findings** - one section per finding, each with the evidence
4. **Indicators of compromise** - hashes, IPs, domains, paths, account names
5. **ATT&CK mapping** - technique IDs against your findings
6. **Recommendations** - containment, eradication, and preventive measures

**Two habits that reliably gain marks:** separate observation from inference
throughout, and state your confidence with what would change it. A grader can tell
immediately whether a candidate understands the difference between "the log shows
X" and "therefore Y happened", and very few do it explicitly.

```text
  - No ATT&CK mapping

  - Roughly ten hours remain
```

What do you do?

</div>
<div slot="model">

**Stop working the lateral movement question. Start the report. Now.**

This is a time-allocation decision, and the arithmetic is unambiguous: you have
ten hours, four substantial deliverables outstanding, and three hours already sunk
into one gap with nothing to show. Continuing is the sunk cost fallacy in exam
form.

**The order I would work:**

**1. Write the report skeleton immediately (30 minutes).** All six sections
headed, with the findings you already have slotted in as bullet points. This does
two things: it makes the remaining work visible, and it guarantees that whatever
happens you submit something structured rather than a pile of notes.

**2. Compile the IOC list (30 minutes).** You already have the material - hashes,
paths, registry keys, the sender address. It is fast, it is definitely marked, and
it requires no new investigation. Take the certain marks first.

**3. Do the ATT&CK mapping (30 minutes).** Same reasoning. You know the techniques
for everything you have found. Fast, marked, no new investigation needed.

**4. Spend two hours on exfiltration (hours 15-17).** This is a completely
unexplored area, so the expected return per hour is far higher than the fourth
hour on lateral movement. Look at SRUM, proxy logs, network telemetry, and any
archive creation - the Phase 4 direction-ratio thinking. Even a partial finding
here is worth more than solving the lateral movement puzzle.

**5. Return to lateral movement for one hour maximum (hour 17-18), with a
different approach.** Not more of the same searching. Ask what you have not
looked at: Prefetch and Amcache on HOST-B to establish what ran and when; the
service binary's creation time in the USN Journal; type-3 logons in whatever event
data survives; SMB share access events; scheduled tasks. **The corroboration habit
from the prep step is exactly what breaks this kind of deadlock** - if the
authentication evidence is missing, find the artefact that records the same
activity by a different mechanism.

**6. Write properly from hour 18 to 22**, review to 23, submit with an hour spare.

**And here is the part that actually gains marks: write up the gap honestly.**

Do not omit lateral movement because you could not fully evidence it. Write:

> *"HOST-B shows a service created at 22:15 with a binary path matching the
> payload identified on HOST-A, indicating the attacker moved between them.
> Investigation did not establish the specific authentication or transfer
> mechanism. Checked: Security event log (cleared at 22:41), SMB share access
> events (not present in the collection), scheduled task creation. Recommend
> examining HOST-B memory and the $MFT for the binary's creation timestamp to
> narrow the transfer window. Moderate confidence that transfer occurred between
> 22:12 and 22:15 based on the payload's creation time on HOST-A."*

That paragraph demonstrates you identified the movement, investigated it
systematically, know what you checked, know what you did not have, and can propose
the next step. **In a real SOC that is a perfectly good handover.** A grader
assessing analytical capability values it far above silence, and it is worth more
than a fourth hour of unproductive searching would have been.

**The transferable lesson:** in any time-boxed investigation, take the certain
marks first, allocate remaining time by expected return rather than by which
puzzle is most annoying, and document gaps rather than hiding them. That is not
exam technique - it is how you work a real incident with a deadline.
