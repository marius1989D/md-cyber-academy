<Why asked="Talk me through your portfolio.">
You have done the work. The risk now is that nobody can see it. A recruiter
screening forty applications gives each one somewhere between twenty and ninety
seconds, and a hiring manager will click at most two links. Everything in this
step is about making those seconds count - not by adding new work, but by
organising what exists so its value is legible to someone who was not there.
</Why>

## The sixty-second test

Open your portfolio in a private browser window and time yourself. Within sixty
seconds a stranger should be able to answer:

1. **What can this person do?**
2. **What evidence supports that?**
3. **What would I ask them about in an interview?**

If any answer requires scrolling past the first screen or opening a second page,
restructure. This is the only test that matters, and almost every entry-level
portfolio fails it by burying the good work three clicks deep.

## Repo structure

One repository, clearly organised. Not five scattered ones.

```text
md-cyber-range/
├── README.md                    ← does all the work
├── 01-lab/
│   ├── architecture.md          diagram, build, cost controls
│   └── blind-spots.md           what this lab cannot see
├── 02-telemetry/
│   └── technique-telemetry.md   the ATT&CK table from Phase 5
├── 03-detections/
│   ├── README.md                methodology and validation approach
│   ├── kql/                     rules, one file each
│   ├── sigma/                   portable versions
│   └── ads/                     ADS documents per rule
├── 04-automation/
│   └── playbooks/               exported Logic Apps + screenshots
└── 05-investigations/
    └── *.md                     redacted write-ups
```

<Callout type="key">
**The README is the portfolio.** Most visitors read it and nothing else, so it
must stand alone: what this is, what you built, what you found, what you would do
differently. Lead with a short summary and a diagram, then link into the detail.
A repo whose README says "see folders" has wasted the click.
</Callout>

**In the README, include numbers.** "Wrote 12 detections across 8 ATT&CK
techniques, validated against 31 atomics, with 3 documented blind spots" is
concrete. "Built a home lab and wrote some detections" is not.

## The GitHub Pages site

Free, and it demonstrates you can use git and a static site generator - a small
signal, but a real one.

Keep it to four pages: **home** (who you are, three lines, and the three strongest
links), **projects** (the lab, with the diagram), **writing** (the four posts), and
**contact** (LinkedIn, GitHub, email). Nothing else. A portfolio site with a blog
categories sidebar and a tag cloud and eleven navigation items reads as someone
performing professionalism rather than demonstrating it.

## What to leave out

As important as what to include:

- **Course completion certificates** other than the credentials themselves. Nobody
  cares about a Udemy certificate.
- **CTF flags with no write-up.** The write-up is the value; the flag is not.
- **Anything from a real employer or customer**, even redacted, unless you have
  explicit written permission. This is the VirusTotal principle from Phase 4 -
  discretion is itself a hiring signal, and its absence is disqualifying.
- **Tutorial follow-alongs presented as original work.** If you followed a guide,
  say so and describe what you changed or found.
- **Half-finished projects.** Three complete things beat eight abandoned ones.

**The two paragraphs that make this post work** are the executive summary and the
"what I got wrong first" section. Everyone can produce technical detail; almost
nobody writes a plain-English impact statement or admits a corrected hypothesis.
Those two are what a hiring manager remembers.

**Before publishing:** verify every claim against your evidence. A confident
statement you cannot support is the one thing that will be found in an interview.

**Then log it**, and link it from your CV's projects section by name.

</BlogBrief>

## The fourth post

Blog post 4 - the career-change narrative - belongs in Phase 7 alongside the CV and
LinkedIn work, because it is a campaign asset rather than a technical one. Note it
now so the writing is planned, but write it when you build the narrative.
