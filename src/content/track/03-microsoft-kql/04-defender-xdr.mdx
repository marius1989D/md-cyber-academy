<Why asked="How do Defender XDR and Sentinel relate, and when would you use each?">
Most UK enterprises run both, and candidates routinely confuse them or assume one
replaces the other. Knowing the division of labour - and being able to trace a
single attack across email, endpoint and identity in one query - is the practical
skill that Phase 4's triage work depends on. This is also where the SC-200 spends
a large share of its objectives, so the exam and the job point the same way.
</Why>

## The four pillars

Defender XDR unifies four products. Each contributes signal and its own hunting
tables:

| Pillar | Covers | Key tables |
|---|---|---|
| **Defender for Endpoint** | Devices - process, file, network, registry | `DeviceProcessEvents`, `DeviceNetworkEvents`, `DeviceFileEvents`, `DeviceRegistryEvents`, `DeviceLogonEvents`, `DeviceImageLoadEvents`, `DeviceEvents` |
| **Defender for Office 365** | Email and collaboration | `EmailEvents`, `EmailAttachmentInfo`, `EmailUrlInfo`, `EmailPostDeliveryEvents`, `UrlClickEvents` |
| **Defender for Identity** | On-prem AD signals | `IdentityLogonEvents`, `IdentityDirectoryEvents`, `IdentityQueryEvents`, `IdentityInfo` |
| **Defender for Cloud Apps** | SaaS activity | `CloudAppEvents` |

Plus the cross-cutting alert tables: **`AlertInfo`** (the alert itself) and
**`AlertEvidence`** (the entities involved). Joining those two is how you pivot
from "an alert fired" to "here is every account, device, file and IP it touched".

<Callout type="key">
`EmailPostDeliveryEvents` and `UrlClickEvents` are the two most under-used tables
in the whole set. Post-delivery records what happened to a message *after* it
landed - including ZAP retracting it once it was reclassified. `UrlClickEvents`
records whether a user actually clicked a Safe Links URL, and what the verdict
was. In a phishing investigation, "did anyone click?" is the question that decides
severity, and this table answers it directly.
</Callout>

## Defender XDR and Sentinel - the division of labour

Both are Microsoft, both do detection, and they overlap. The honest framing:

**Defender XDR** is deep, first-party telemetry across Microsoft workloads, with
built-in correlation. It knows a device, a mailbox and an identity are related
because it owns all three. Retention on the hunting tables is limited - typically
30 days.

**Sentinel** is the broad SIEM: it ingests everything else too - firewalls, AWS,
network appliances, third-party tooling - with configurable long retention, custom
analytics rules, and SOAR.

**Typical architecture:** Defender does the deep Microsoft-workload work and
forwards its alerts and incidents to Sentinel, which correlates them with
everything non-Microsoft and holds the long tail of data. The two are increasingly
surfaced in a single unified portal, which blurs the boundary in the interface but
not in the architecture.

**The practical consequence for you:** the same KQL runs in both, but **table names
differ**. Endpoint data is `DeviceProcessEvents` in Defender advanced hunting and,
if you connect the connector, also lands in Sentinel. Windows Security events
collected by an agent land in `SecurityEvent`. Knowing which table lives where is
a recurring source of confusion and a fair interview question.

## Custom detections

In Defender you can promote an advanced hunting query into a **custom detection
rule** - it runs on a schedule, raises alerts, and can trigger automated response
actions (isolate device, restrict app execution, disable user). Requirements worth
remembering: the query must return `Timestamp` and a device or user identifier
column so the alert can be attributed to an entity.

This is the Defender-side equivalent of the hunt-to-detection lifecycle you
practised in the last step.

## Response actions

Available from the portal or via API, and worth naming because they show you
understand where automation ends and authority begins:

- **Device:** isolate (full or selective), run antivirus scan, restrict app
  execution, collect investigation package, live response session
- **File:** quarantine, block by hash across the estate
- **Identity:** disable user, force password reset, require re-authentication

<Callout type="warn">
Selective isolation keeps the Defender connection alive so you retain visibility
and live-response capability while cutting everything else. It is almost always
the right choice over full isolation during an active investigation - full
isolation blinds you too. This distinction is a good detail to raise.
</Callout>

## Challenge - trace a phishing attack end to end

<QueryChallenge
  step="track/03-microsoft-kql/04-defender-xdr"
  id="phish-to-endpoint"
  title="Email to click to execution"
  lang="kql"
  tables={["EmailEvents", "EmailUrlInfo", "UrlClickEvents", "DeviceProcessEvents", "NetworkMessageId", "RecipientEmailAddress", "AccountUpn"]}
>
<div slot="brief">

A phishing campaign was reported. Build a single query that establishes, for the
last 7 days: who received it, who actually clicked the link, and whether
suspicious process execution followed on that user's device within an hour.

Assume the campaign is identifiable by a sender domain of `docsign-review.com`.

</div>
<div slot="solution">

```kql
let campaign = materialize(
    EmailEvents
    | where Timestamp > ago(7d)
    | where SenderFromDomain =~ "docsign-review.com"
    | project NetworkMessageId, EmailTimestamp = Timestamp,
              RecipientEmailAddress, Subject, DeliveryAction,
              SenderFromAddress
);
let clicks =
    UrlClickEvents
    | where Timestamp > ago(7d)
    | project ClickTime = Timestamp, NetworkMessageId,
              AccountUpn, Url, ActionType, IsClickedThrough;
let execs =
    DeviceProcessEvents
    | where Timestamp > ago(7d)
    | where InitiatingProcessFileName in~
        ("outlook.exe","msedge.exe","chrome.exe","winword.exe","excel.exe")
    | where FileName in~
        ("powershell.exe","cmd.exe","wscript.exe","cscript.exe",
         "mshta.exe","rundll32.exe","regsvr32.exe")
    | extend Account = tolower(AccountName)
    | project ExecTime = Timestamp, Account, DeviceName,
              FileName, ProcessCommandLine, InitiatingProcessFileName;
campaign
| join kind=leftouter clicks on NetworkMessageId
| extend Account = tolower(tostring(split(RecipientEmailAddress, "@")[0]))
| join kind=leftouter execs on Account
| where isempty(ExecTime)
     or ExecTime between (ClickTime .. ClickTime + 1h)
| project EmailTimestamp, RecipientEmailAddress, Subject, DeliveryAction,
          Clicked = isnotempty(ClickTime), ClickTime, IsClickedThrough,
          DeviceName, ExecTime, InitiatingProcessFileName,
          FileName, ProcessCommandLine
| sort by EmailTimestamp asc
```

**Why it is built this way:**

- **`NetworkMessageId` is the join key between email and click.** It is the unique
  identifier for a message across the Office 365 tables, and it is the reason you
  can connect a delivery to a click at all. Knowing this field by name is a strong
  signal.
- **`leftouter` on clicks, not `inner`.** You need the recipients who *did not*
  click as well - that is the denominator for scoping, and it tells you who still
  needs warning.
- **`IsClickedThrough`** distinguishes a user who hit the Safe Links warning page
  and stopped from one who clicked past it. Materially different severity for that
  individual.
- **The account normalisation again** - `RecipientEmailAddress` is a full address,
  `AccountName` on the device side is not. Same fix as the previous step.
- **`isempty(ExecTime) or ExecTime between (...)`** keeps rows where nothing
  executed, so a single result set answers all three questions rather than
  silently dropping the clean recipients.

**Common wrong shapes:** using `inner` joins throughout, which returns only the
worst-affected users and makes the campaign look far smaller than it is; joining
email to device on address without normalising; and filtering the execution window
before the join, when `ClickTime` is not yet in scope.

**In practice you would extend this** with `EmailAttachmentInfo` if the campaign
carried a file, and `EmailPostDeliveryEvents` to see whether ZAP retracted
anything after the fact. And the output feeds straight into a Phase 4 escalation:
delivered count, clicked count, clicked-through count, and execution count are
exactly the four numbers an incident manager will ask for.

</div>
</QueryChallenge>
